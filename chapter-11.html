Chapter 11 — Real Estate Contracts

What this chapter is. Almost everything a sales associate does for money happens through a contract: the listing agreement that hires the brokerage, the sales contract between buyer and seller, an option, a lease. This chapter is general contract law, then the Florida rules that sit on top of it — the statute of frauds, the limits on what a licensee may write without practicing law, Florida's seller-disclosure duty, and the standard Florida Realtors/Florida Bar contracts. The exam tests vocabulary that sounds alike (void versus voidable, bilateral versus unilateral, executed versus executory), the exact moment a contract is formed, and which remedy fits which breach.

Where a number below is stated, it is certain. Where it says "under Florida law" without a number, take the number from the textbook.

What a contract is, and how contracts are classified

A contract is an agreement between competent parties, supported by consideration, to do or not do something legal. The exam classifies contracts four ways:

  • Express or implied. An express contract is stated in words, written or spoken. An implied contract is inferred from conduct — a person who keeps accepting a broker's services and benefiting from them may be bound even without saying so.
  • Bilateral or unilateral. A bilateral contract is a promise for a promise: the seller promises to sell, the buyer promises to buy. A unilateral contract is a promise for an act: only one side is bound unless and until the other side performs. An open listing ("I'll pay whichever broker brings a buyer") and an option contract are the standard unilateral examples.
  • Executed or executory. An executed contract has been fully performed by everyone. An executory contract still has something left to do. A signed sales contract is executory until closing.
  • Valid, void, voidable, unenforceable — covered in its own section below.

The trap: "executed" in everyday speech means signed. In contract law it means fully performed. A contract signed by both parties on Loquat Street last week, with closing next month, is executory, not executed. The deed, once delivered, is the executed piece.

The essential elements

For a contract to be valid, it needs:

  1. Competent parties. Of legal age (18 in Florida) and of sound mind. A contract with a minor is generally voidable by the minor. A contract with someone already declared legally incompetent by a court is void.
  2. Offer and acceptance — a meeting of the minds (mutual assent) on the same terms.
  3. Consideration — something of value exchanged by each side. A promise counts. In real estate it is usually the purchase price for the property, with the deposit as evidence of the buyer's seriousness, not the consideration itself.
  4. Legal purpose. A contract to do something illegal is void.
  5. For real estate, a writing signed by the party to be charged, under the statute of frauds (next section).

The trap: the deposit is not required for a valid contract. A buyer on Mangrove Bend who signs a contract with no earnest money still has a valid contract, because the mutual promises are the consideration. The deposit protects the seller; it does not create the deal.

The statute of frauds

The statute of frauds requires certain contracts to be in writing and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought, or they cannot be enforced in court. In Florida the general statute is Section 725.01 of the Florida Statutes, and it covers, among other things, contracts for the sale of land or any interest in land, and leases for a term of more than one year. Section 689.01 separately requires conveyances and leases of more than one year to be in writing and signed with two witnesses.

What this means in practice:

  • A contract to buy or sell real property must be in writing to be enforceable.
  • A lease for one year or less may be oral. A lease for more than one year must be written.
  • An oral contract that falls under the statute is not illegal or void. It is unenforceable — the parties can still perform it voluntarily, but neither can make the other do so in court.

Florida does not require a listing agreement to be in writing to be valid, but a written listing has to meet the requirements of Chapter 475 (covered below), and a broker trying to collect a commission on an oral listing faces a much harder case.

The trap: the oral handshake deal on Hibiscus Avenue. Seller and buyer agree out loud on price and closing date, and the seller then backs out. The buyer cannot enforce it — not because it is void, but because it is unenforceable under the statute of frauds. If both sides had gone ahead and closed, nothing would be wrong with the sale.

Offer, acceptance, and when the contract exists

An offer is a proposal that shows a willingness to be bound on specific terms. The person making it is the offeror; the person receiving it is the offeree.

Acceptance has to match the offer exactly and be communicated to the offeror. In real estate, a contract is formed when the offeree accepts, in writing, without changes, and that acceptance is communicated back to the offeror. Signing alone, without communication, does not complete it.

Any change to any term — price, closing date, which appliances stay — is not an acceptance. It is a counteroffer, which rejects the original offer and creates a new one, with the roles reversed. The original offer is gone; the original offeror can accept the counteroffer, reject it, or counter again, but cannot go back and accept the first version.

An offer ends by:

  • Acceptance (it becomes a contract);
  • Rejection or counteroffer by the offeree;
  • Revocation by the offeror any time before acceptance is communicated, even if the offer said it would stay open, unless the offeree paid for that promise (which makes it an option);
  • Lapse of time — the offer's own deadline passes, or a reasonable time passes if none was set;
  • Death or insanity of either party before acceptance.

The Florida Realtors/Florida Bar contracts define an Effective Date: the date on which the last of the parties signed or initialed and delivered the final offer or counteroffer. All of the contract's deadlines count from that date.

The trap: the seller on Firebush Court signs the buyer's offer but changes the closing date by a week and initials the change. That is a counteroffer, not an acceptance, and until the buyer initials the change and it is delivered back, there is no contract. If a second buyer's better offer arrives in the meantime, the seller is free to take it — and the first buyer is free to walk.

Valid, void, voidable, unenforceable

  • Valid: all essential elements are present; it is binding and enforceable.
  • Void: it was never a contract — an essential element is missing, such as a legal purpose. Neither side can enforce it.
  • Voidable: valid on its face, but one party has the right to cancel it. Contracts with minors, contracts induced by fraud or misrepresentation, and contracts signed under duress are voidable by the injured party. The statutory cancellation rights in Chapter 8 — the condominium, HOA and timeshare periods — make those contracts voidable by the buyer during the window.
  • Unenforceable: valid between the parties, but a court will not enforce it — usually because it is oral when it needed to be written, or because the time to sue has run out.

In Florida, the time to sue on a written contract is 5 years and on an oral contract is 4 years, under Section 95.11.

The trap: void and voidable. A contract with a 16-year-old on Poinsettia Drive is voidable at the minor's option, not void. The minor can keep it or cancel it; the adult cannot. A contract to use the property for an illegal purpose is void, and no one can enforce it.

Licensees and the practice of law

Florida licensees may fill in the blanks on standardized contract forms — the forms prepared and approved by the Florida Realtors and The Florida Bar are the main ones — as part of a real estate transaction they are handling. Licensees may not draft contracts from scratch, write custom legal clauses, or give legal advice about the effect of the terms. Doing so is the unauthorized practice of law.

When a client needs something the form does not cover — an unusual contingency, a complicated title problem, a question about what a clause means legally — the licensee's job is to recommend an attorney.

The trap: a buyer on Loquat Street asks the associate to "just add a paragraph" letting him back out if his business deal falls through. Choosing a pre-printed addendum from the approved forms is fine. Writing a new legal clause in the margin is not. The exam treats the hand-written custom clause as the violation.

Listing agreements and buyer brokerage agreements

A listing agreement is the contract between a property owner and a brokerage, setting out the terms on which the brokerage will market the property and earn its fee. It is an employment contract — the broker is hired — and the listing belongs to the broker, not to the sales associate.

The types:

  • Exclusive right of sale: the broker earns the fee no matter who sells the property, even if the owner finds the buyer. The most common and the most protective of the broker.
  • Exclusive agency: the broker earns the fee if anyone other than the owner sells; if the owner finds the buyer personally, no fee is owed.
  • Open listing: the owner may list with any number of brokers and pays only the one who produces the buyer. The owner owes nothing if they sell it themselves.
  • Net listing: the broker keeps whatever the sale price exceeds a set net amount for the seller. It invites conflicts of interest; take Florida's treatment of net listings from the book.

Under Section 475.25(1)(r), any written listing agreement in Florida must include:

  • a definite expiration date;
  • a description of the property;
  • the price and terms;
  • the fee or commission;
  • the signature of the principal.

The licensee must give the principal a legible, signed, true and correct copy within 24 hours of obtaining the written listing. The agreement may not contain a provision requiring the owner to notify the broker of an intent to cancel after the expiration date — in other words, no automatic renewal that the owner has to opt out of.

Buyer brokerage agreements do the same job on the buyer's side: they set out the brokerage's duties to the buyer and how the brokerage will be paid. Industry rules adopted after the 2024 national commission settlement have made written buyer agreements standard practice before touring homes; take the current Florida form and the course's treatment from the book.

The trap: a listing on Mangrove Bend that runs "for 90 days and renews automatically each month until the seller cancels in writing." That clause violates Florida law because it requires the seller to notify the broker after the expiration date. The other trap is the 24 hours: it runs from obtaining the signed listing, not from the end of the business day.

The sales contract: the Florida Realtors/Florida Bar forms

Most Florida residential sales use one of two forms jointly approved by Florida Realtors and The Florida Bar:

  • The standard Residential Contract for Sale and Purchase ("FR/BAR"), in which the seller has repair obligations up to limits the parties write in, for problems found during inspections.
  • The "AS IS" Residential Contract for Sale and Purchase ("FR/BAR AS IS"), in which the seller makes no repair commitment, and the buyer has an inspection period during which the buyer may cancel in the buyer's sole discretion if the inspections are not acceptable, and get the deposit back.

The pieces of either form the exam will ask about:

  • Parties and property, including the legal description and the personal property included or excluded.
  • Purchase price and deposit. The form sets deadlines, counted from the Effective Date, for delivering the initial and any additional deposit to the escrow agent named in the contract. The escrow agent's handling of the money is governed by the Chapter 5 rules.
  • Financing. If the contract is contingent on financing, the buyer has a set period to obtain loan approval and must apply promptly and in good faith; the buyer who misses the deadline or fails to cancel in time may lose the protection.
  • Closing date, and time is of the essence — a clause that makes every stated deadline binding rather than approximate.
  • Title evidence and survey (Chapter 9), and how title defects are cured.
  • Inspections, and in the standard form, the seller's repair obligations up to the stated limits.
  • Risk of loss if the property is damaged before closing.
  • Default clauses — what each side gets if the other breaches.

Both forms have their own rules for counting days and for deadlines that fall on a weekend or holiday; read them in the form rather than assuming.

Once a sales contract is signed, the buyer holds equitable title — an interest in the property that a court will protect — while the seller keeps legal title until the deed is delivered at closing.

The trap: a buyer under the AS IS form on Hibiscus Avenue wants to cancel because the inspection found nothing wrong, but he has changed his mind about the neighborhood. During the inspection period, under the AS IS form, he may cancel in his sole discretion and get his deposit back. After the inspection period ends, that right is gone. The deadline matters more than the reason.

Florida's disclosure requirements

Florida does not have a single statutory seller-disclosure form, but it has a strong disclosure duty.

Johnson v. Davis (1985). The Florida Supreme Court held that a seller of residential property who knows of facts that materially affect the value of the property, that are not readily observable, and that are not known to the buyer, has a duty to disclose them. A leaking roof concealed behind fresh paint is the classic example. An "as is" clause does not excuse a seller from this duty. Licensees have their own disclosure duty under Chapter 475 for known facts that materially affect value and are not readily observable.

On top of that, several specific disclosures are required by statute or federal law:

  • Radon gas. Florida requires a specific radon notice, in the language set by Section 404.056, in contracts for the sale and lease of buildings. The standard forms include it.
  • Property tax disclosure. Section 689.261 requires a disclosure warning the buyer not to rely on the seller's current property taxes, because a sale can trigger a reassessment and the loss of the seller's homestead exemption and Save Our Homes cap.
  • Lead-based paint. For housing built before 1978, federal law requires a lead-based paint disclosure, the EPA pamphlet, and an opportunity for the buyer to conduct a risk assessment, which defaults to 10 days unless the parties agree otherwise.
  • Community and ownership disclosures from Chapter 8: the condominium documents, the HOA disclosure summary, and the CDD disclosure.
  • Energy efficiency. Florida requires that a buyer of real property with a building be given the state's energy-efficiency rating brochure at or before contract signing; take the specifics from the book.
  • Coastal construction control line. For property partly or fully seaward of the state's coastal construction control line, the seller must disclose that fact to the buyer; take the timing and specifics from the book.

The trap: a seller on Firebush Court who had the attic replaced after termite damage two years ago, then repainted, and says nothing because the contract is AS IS. Under Johnson v. Davis that seller had a duty to disclose a known, material, hidden defect. "As is" protects the seller from repair demands, not from concealment.

Options and rights of first refusal

An option contract gives one person the right to buy property at a set price within a set time, without the obligation to buy. The owner is the optionor; the person holding the right is the optionee. The optionee pays option consideration for the right, and the optionor is bound to sell on the stated terms if the optionee exercises it within the period.

Until exercise, an option is unilateral — only the optionor is bound. When the optionee exercises, it becomes a bilateral contract of sale. Because the optionor is bound to keep the offer open, the optionor cannot revoke during the option period.

A right of first refusal is different: it gives the holder the right to match an offer the owner receives from someone else, if and when the owner decides to sell. The owner is not obligated to sell at all.

The trap: a tenant on Poinsettia Drive with an option at $410,000 versus a tenant with a right of first refusal. The option tenant can force the sale at $410,000 within the option period. The right-of-first-refusal tenant can do nothing until the owner gets an offer, then can only match it.

Installment contracts (contract for deed)

In an installment sales contract, also called a contract for deed or land contract, the buyer (vendee) takes possession and pays the price in installments to the seller (vendor), who keeps legal title until the price, or an agreed portion, is paid. The buyer holds equitable title in the meantime.

Florida courts have generally treated these contracts much like mortgages when the buyer defaults, so the seller usually has to go to court rather than simply keep the payments and retake the property. Take the course's treatment of the details from the book.

The trap: assuming the buyer under a contract for deed on Loquat Street owns nothing until the last payment. He holds equitable title and possession. The seller holds only legal title.

Assignment, novation and discharge

Assignment transfers one party's rights under a contract to someone else; the assignor stays liable if the assignee does not perform. Most contracts can be assigned unless they say otherwise. The FR/BAR forms let the parties choose whether the buyer may assign.

Novation replaces a contract or a party with a new one, and releases the original party. A loan assumption with the lender's release of the original borrower is a novation.

A contract is discharged by:

  • Performance — everyone does what they promised;
  • Mutual agreement to cancel (rescission);
  • Impossibility — the property is destroyed, or performance becomes illegal;
  • Operation of law — the statute of limitations runs, a party goes bankrupt, or a court orders it.

The trap: a buyer on Mangrove Bend who assigns her contract to her cousin thinks she is out of the deal. With an assignment, she remains liable if the cousin fails to close. Only a novation releases her.

Breach and remedies

A breach is the failure to perform without a legal excuse. The remedies:

  • Specific performance: a court orders the breaching party to do what they promised — usually to convey the property. Available because every parcel of real estate is considered unique, so money may not be enough. A buyer is the one who usually seeks it.
  • Compensatory damages: money to make the injured party whole.
  • Liquidated damages: an amount the parties agreed to in advance — in residential contracts, usually the deposit. When the contract makes the deposit the seller's sole remedy, the seller who keeps it cannot also sue for more.
  • Rescission: the contract is undone, and each side gets back what they gave.

The FR/BAR forms set out what each side may do on default, and the seller's and buyer's options are not the same. Read the default paragraph in the form.

The trap: the seller on Hibiscus Avenue whose buyer walks away and who wants both the $20,000 deposit and a lawsuit for the full loss. If the contract makes the deposit liquidated damages and the seller elects to keep it, that is the whole remedy.

How to use this chapter

Read the classification, formation and void-versus-voidable sections before opening the chapter; those are the definitions the exam uses most. Then read the listing and disclosure sections right before those parts of the chapter, and keep a copy of the FR/BAR AS IS contract open while reading the contract section — the form is the real thing the exam is describing.
