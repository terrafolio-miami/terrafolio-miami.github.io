Callback: a single agent's confidentiality survives the end of the relationship, but it never covers a known material defect.

Conflicting demands on an escrow deposit

A deal collapses and both sides claim the deposit. The buyer says the inspection let him out; the seller says he walked and the money is hers. The broker holding it has no authority to decide who is right.

Under Florida law the broker may not release escrow funds on his own judgment. He must notify FREC in writing within the time Florida law sets, then use one of the authorized settlement procedures: mediation or arbitration if both parties agree, an escrow disbursement order from FREC, or interpleader, where a court decides. Escrow money is never the brokerage's to allocate.

The trap: doing nothing is itself the violation. A buyer and seller on Heron Bay Drive both claim a $15,000 deposit, the broker decides to sit tight until they sort it out, and a year later it is still in the account. No notice, no procedure — that is the disciplinable act, regardless of who was actually entitled. Releasing it to whoever pushes hardest is the other wrong answer. The broker's job is to escalate, not to judge.

Pointer: the conflicting demands part of Chapter 5. ($15,000 is a scenario amount.)

2026-10-20

Callback: a licensee's opinion of value is a CMA, not an appraisal — and renaming the file doesn't fix a document dressed up as one.

Confidentiality, and what it survives

A single agent owes the principal confidentiality: the seller's motivation, the lowest price they would actually take, the divorce, the job transfer. None of it goes to the other side. Under Florida law that duty survives the end of the relationship — the listing expiring does not release you.

A transaction broker owes only limited confidentiality: it keeps back that the seller would take less, or that the buyer would pay more. With no brokerage relationship you owe none of it, though honesty and material disclosure still apply.

The trap: the exam dresses it up as helpfulness. Your seller on Palmetto Lane listed at $400,000 and told you she would take $370,000. The listing expires in March. In June a buyer asks what she would have taken and you tell him, because you are not her agent anymore. Still a breach.

The hard edge the other way: confidentiality never covers a known material defect. You may not keep a seller's secret about the roof. Price and motive are confidential; facts about the property are not.

Pointer: the fiduciary duties part of Chapter 4. (Dollar figures are scenario amounts.)

2026-10-19

Callback: a sales associate is a general agent of the broker, with continuing authority across the brokerage's business, while a broker taking one listing is a special agent of that seller.

Comparative market analysis vs. appraisal

When you price a house for a seller, you are doing a comparative market analysis: recent nearby sales, adjusted for differences, landing on a likely range. Under Florida law a licensee may prepare an opinion of value like this as part of ordinary brokerage practice. What you may not do is present it as an appraisal. Appraisal is separately licensed and separately regulated in Florida, with its own standards.

The reason is who relies on it. A CMA helps a seller pick a list price. An appraisal is what a lender leans on before putting money on the property.

The trap: the exam gives you a licensee who works up comps for 412 Oak Street, types "Appraisal Report" across the top, and charges the seller $300. Getting paid is not the problem. The label is — it tells the reader an appraiser's standards were followed, which crosses into work the license does not cover. And renaming the file "market analysis" does not save a document that still reads like an appraisal.

Pointer: the opinion-of-value part of Chapter 5. ($300 is a scenario amount.)

2026-10-16

Callback: every brokerage ad has to carry the registered brokerage name — a personal or team brand can never stand alone.

General, special, and universal agents

These three are about scope of authority, not importance. A universal agent can act for the principal in essentially anything — rare in real estate, closer to a full power of attorney. A general agent acts for the principal across a continuing course of business, with ongoing authority in many transactions. A special agent is hired for one specific task, and that authority ends when the task does.

A sales associate is a general agent of the broker. The relationship is continuous: you act for the brokerage day after day, across every listing and every deal, which is why under Florida law the broker is held responsible for what you do. A broker who takes a single listing, by contrast, is a special agent of that seller — authority over that one property, that one sale.

The trap: students flip it. Each listing feels like one job, so the sales associate gets labeled a special agent. Or "general" gets read as broad personal power — a universal agent — when it only means ongoing authority inside the broker's business.

Pointer: the types of agents part of Chapter 4.

--- Follow-up the user asked for (worked examples): Universal agent — a full durable power of attorney: an elderly owner signs one, and her son can list the house, sign the contract, move the money, handle unrelated business too. General agent — ongoing authority across a continuing course of business: a sales associate for their broker; a property manager who collects rent, signs leases and hires the plumber year after year. Special agent — one task, authority ends with it: a broker taking the listing on 412 Oak Street; a buyer's broker hired to find one home; an attorney-in-fact under a one-time limited power of attorney to sign a single deed. The flip to watch: the associate handles individual listings but is still a general agent, because the authority runs to the broker's business, not to any one deal.

Advertising in your own name

Every ad you run belongs to the brokerage, not to you. Under Florida law, brokerage advertising has to include the registered name of the brokerage in a way a reader can actually see — not buried, not abbreviated into something unrecognizable.

That is because you have no independent right to solicit business. A sales associate's authority flows from the broker, so an ad that names only you implies a brokerage that does not exist.

The trap: the exam builds it out of personal branding, because that is where real agents get caught. A team name, a catchy slogan with your cell number, a logo you had made — none of those can stand alone. Your own Instagram post about a listing is advertising. So is a yard-sign rider with just your name and number on it. A personal name or team brand is allowed alongside the registered brokerage name, never instead of it. And the broker is accountable for all of it, which is why your ads are their business.

Pointer: the advertising part of Chapter 5.

2026-10-14

Callback: a broker's own operating or personal account is never an escrow depository, no matter how briefly the money sits there.

Obedience, and where it stops

A single agent owes the principal obedience: you follow the seller's lawful instructions, even ones you think are bad business. Price it too high, reject every offer under a number, only show it on weekends — that is theirs to decide, not yours.

Under Florida law obedience covers lawful instructions only. An unlawful instruction is not a gray area; you refuse it, and if the seller insists, you withdraw. "The seller told me to" has never been a defense in a FREC complaint, because the license is yours, not his.

The trap: the exam pairs obedience against disclosure. A seller tells you not to mention that the roof leaks, or asks you to steer certain buyers away. Both are instructions. Neither is lawful. Full disclosure to your principal does not license concealment from the customer — known material defects affecting residential value still have to be disclosed, and refusing a buyer on a protected basis is a fair housing violation whoever ordered it. The duty you breach by obeying is honesty, and obedience never excuses it.

Pointer: the fiduciary duties part of Chapter 4.

2026-10-13

Callback: helpfulness is not representation — a buyer who feels looked after is still a customer, and only a written single agency makes someone a principal.

Who may hold escrow funds in Florida

Under Florida law, when a broker holds a deposit it has to go into a separate escrow or trust account at a bank, savings association, credit union, or trust company located in Florida. That account holds other people's money and nothing else.

The broker also has the option of not holding it. The deposit can instead be held by a title company with trust powers or by an attorney. What the broker cannot do is lose track of it.

Florida is strict here because escrow money is the public's, not the brokerage's. A regulated Florida depository keeps those funds reachable by the parties and by FREC.

The trap: the broker's own operating account is never an escrow depository, and neither is a personal account, no matter how briefly the money sits there. The other version is the buyer who wants to hand his deposit to a friendly title agent instead. That is permitted, but the broker still has to verify in writing that the funds actually arrived. "I assume the title company has it" is not compliance.

Pointer: the escrow depository part of Chapter 5.

2026-10-12

Callback: a sales associate can be paid for brokerage services only by their employing broker — not the seller, not the other side's brokerage.

Single agency: the principal and the customer

When a broker takes a listing as a single agent, the seller is the principal — the one owed loyalty, confidentiality, obedience, full disclosure, accounting, and skill, care and diligence. Everyone else in that deal is a customer. A customer is someone you deal with honestly. A principal is someone you work for.

Under Florida law this is why the unrepresented buyer is the exam's favorite victim. He calls the listing licensee, walks the house with them, and starts talking: his top number, his divorce, how fast he needs to close. He feels represented because someone is being helpful. He is a customer, and every word he says about price and motive can go straight to the seller — the licensee owes the seller full disclosure of it.

The trap: helpfulness is not representation, and the buyer's belief does not create agency. Florida requires single agency to be disclosed in writing precisely because that assumption is so predictable. Being nice to a customer never converts them into a principal; only a written single agency does.

Pointer: the single agency part of Chapter 4.

2026-10-09

Callback: Florida presumes you're a transaction broker unless a single agency or no-brokerage relationship is put in writing — you become a transaction broker by accident, never a single agent.

A sales associate may be paid only by their employing broker

Under Florida law, a sales associate cannot collect compensation for brokerage services from anyone except the broker they work for. Not the seller, not the buyer, not the other side's brokerage, not the title company. The commission belongs to the broker, and the broker pays the associate out of it.

The reason is accountability. A sales associate is a general agent of the broker, and the broker is the one FREC holds responsible for every dollar and every act in the transaction. If money could reach the associate directly, the broker would have no way to supervise what they are licensed to supervise.

The trap: the exam dresses this up as gratitude or convenience. A grateful seller at closing hands the associate a $500 check as a thank-you. A co-operating broker writes the associate's half of the commission straight to them to save a step. Both are violations, and both sound harmless in the question. The money has to go through the employing broker — intent and goodwill change nothing.

Pointer: the compensation part of Chapter 5. ($500 is a scenario amount.)

Single agent, transaction broker, no brokerage relationship

These are the three authorized relationships in Florida, and the exam cares most about which one you fall into by default. Under Florida law, a licensee is presumed to be operating as a transaction broker unless a single agency or no-brokerage relationship is established in writing with the customer. You don't become a single agent by accident. You become a transaction broker by accident.

A single agent represents one party with full fiduciary duties: loyalty, confidentiality, obedience, full disclosure, accounting for all funds, and skill, care and diligence. A transaction broker gives limited representation to both sides — honesty and fair dealing, accounting, skill, care and diligence, limited confidentiality, presenting all offers — but no loyalty and no obedience. No brokerage relationship means you represent nobody in the deal.

The trap: "no brokerage relationship" sounds like no duties, and that is wrong. In all three you still owe honest and fair dealing, accounting for funds, and disclosure of known facts that materially affect the value of residential property and are not readily observable. The relationship changes loyalty, not honesty.

Pointer: the authorized relationships part of Chapter 4.
