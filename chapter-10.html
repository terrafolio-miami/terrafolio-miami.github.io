Chapter 10 — Legal Descriptions

What this chapter is. A deed has to describe the land precisely enough that a surveyor, using only the words on the page, could walk out and find it. That description is the legal description, and this chapter covers the three systems Florida uses to write one: metes and bounds, the government (rectangular) survey system, and lot and block. It is the most arithmetic-heavy chapter in the first half of the course. The exam asks you to read a description, place it on a grid, and compute its acreage, and it uses a handful of conversion numbers that are worth knowing cold. Unlike most chapters, the numbers here are fixed measurements, not legal thresholds that change with the legislature.

Why a street address is not enough

A street address tells the mail carrier where to go. It does not tell anyone where the property begins and ends. Streets get renamed, numbers get reassigned, and an address says nothing about the boundary lines. The county property appraiser's parcel identification number is a tax-roll reference, not a legal description either.

A legal description has to identify one parcel and only one, with boundaries that can be located on the ground. A deed whose description is too vague to locate the land is defective, and a contract for sale needs a description adequate to identify the property.

The trap: a contract for 2210 Spanish Moss Trail that lists only the street address and the parcel ID. The contract can still be enforceable if the property can be identified, but the deed at closing needs the full legal description. The exam treats "street address" and "tax parcel number" as wrong answers whenever it asks what a legal description is.

Measurements to memorize

Every calculation in this chapter rests on these:

  • 1 mile = 5,280 feet
  • 1 acre = 43,560 square feet
  • 1 section = 1 square mile = 640 acres
  • 1 township = 6 miles by 6 miles = 36 sections = 36 square miles
  • 1 rod = 16.5 feet
  • 1 chain = 66 feet = 4 rods; 80 chains make a mile
  • A perfectly square acre is about 208.71 feet on a side — the exam sometimes uses this as a distractor rather than a working number

An acre is a measure of area, not a shape. A long, thin lot can be an acre as easily as a square one.

The trap: confusing square feet with feet. An acre is 43,560 square feet. A mile is 5,280 feet in a straight line. A square mile is 640 acres, which is about 27.9 million square feet. Questions that switch between linear and square units are where people lose points.

Metes and bounds

The oldest system, and the one used for irregular parcels that don't fit a grid. "Metes" are measurements — distances and directions. "Bounds" are boundaries — the monuments and landmarks the lines run between.

A metes-and-bounds description reads like walking directions around the edge of the parcel:

  • It starts at a point of beginning (POB) that can be located on the ground.
  • It runs a series of courses (directions) and distances from point to point.
  • It must return to the point of beginning. A description that does not close does not describe an enclosed parcel.

Monuments are fixed points that mark corners or lines: iron pins, concrete markers set by a surveyor, and in older descriptions natural features like a tree or a stream. When the monuments and the stated distances disagree, the monuments generally control, because they are what was actually on the ground when the land was surveyed.

Bearings (directions) are stated as an angle from north or south toward east or west, never more than 90 degrees. "North 45 degrees East" means start facing north and turn 45 degrees toward east. "South 30 degrees West" means start facing south and turn 30 degrees toward west. A bearing of exactly 90 degrees is due east or due west.

Florida descriptions very often start at a section corner from the government survey, which is a known, recorded point, and then run courses and distances from there to the actual corner of the parcel. That first known point is the point of commencement; the parcel's own first corner is the point of beginning. The two are not the same.

The trap: a description for a waterfront parcel on Osprey Ridge Road that starts at "the southwest corner of Section 14, thence North 300 feet to the point of beginning." The southwest corner is where the surveyor started measuring, not a corner of the parcel. The parcel starts 300 feet north. The exam will ask which point is the POB and offer the section corner as an answer.

The government survey system

Also called the rectangular survey system or Public Land Survey System. Created by Congress for land acquired by the federal government, it divides the land into a grid of squares measured from a fixed starting point. Florida was surveyed this way after the United States acquired it from Spain.

The grid starts with two lines that cross at a fixed point:

  • A principal meridian, running north–south.
  • A base line, running east–west.

Florida's starting point is the Tallahassee Meridian and its base line, which intersect in Tallahassee. Every township in Florida is located by counting from that point. Because most of Florida's land lies south and east of Tallahassee, most Florida descriptions say "Township __ South, Range __ East." Parts of the Panhandle west of Tallahassee are in ranges west, and a band of the state north of Tallahassee is in townships north.

Some parts of Florida, notably areas that were old Spanish land grants, were never fully brought into the grid and are described by other means.

The trap: the exam asks which principal meridian governs Florida land. It is the Tallahassee Meridian — not a federal meridian named for some other state, and not a meridian passing through Miami.

Townships and ranges

From the meridian and base line, the land is divided into rows and columns six miles wide.

  • Township lines run east–west, every six miles north and south of the base line. The rows between them are called townships, numbered North or South of the base line: Township 1 South is the first six-mile row below the base line, Township 2 South the next, and so on.
  • Range lines run north–south, every six miles east and west of the principal meridian. The columns between them are ranges, numbered East or West of the meridian: Range 1 East is the first six-mile column east of the meridian.

Where a township row and a range column cross, the resulting six-mile-by-six-mile square is itself called a township — the confusing double use of the word that the exam relies on. "Township 4 South, Range 3 East" identifies one square of 36 square miles by its row and its column.

Because the earth is curved, meridians converge as they move north, and the squares would shrink if nothing corrected them. Correction lines (standard parallels) run east–west every 24 miles, and guide meridians run north–south every 24 miles, to reset the measurements. A 24-mile-by-24-mile block bounded by these is called a check or quadrangle, and it contains 16 townships.

The trap: the word "township." A township line is east–west. A tier or row of townships runs east–west. A township square is the 36-square-mile unit. If a question asks "how many square miles are in a township," the answer is 36; if it asks "how far apart are township lines," the answer is 6 miles.

Sections and their numbering

Each township is divided into 36 sections, each one mile square and containing 640 acres.

The sections are numbered in a back-and-forth pattern that starts in the northeast corner:

  • Section 1 is in the northeast corner. The top row runs right to left (east to west): 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
  • Section 7 is directly below section 6, and the second row runs left to right (west to east): 7 through 12.
  • The pattern continues, snaking back and forth, until section 36 ends in the southeast corner.

So section 6 is in the northwest corner, section 31 is in the southwest corner, and section 36 is in the southeast corner. Adjacent sections across a row boundary are numbered in a way that is easy to check: section 1 sits directly above 12, 12 above 13, 13 above 24, 24 above 25, 25 above 36.

Section 16 was historically set aside to support public schools, which is why some older descriptions or local history refer to the "school section."

Along rivers, lakes and the coast — and in sections that absorb the errors caught by the correction lines — a section may not be a full 640-acre square. The irregular pieces are surveyed as numbered government lots, and a description will say, for example, "Government Lot 3 of Section 7."

The trap: drawing the grid starting in the northwest corner the way people read a page. It starts in the northeast. The exam will ask which section is in the northwest corner (6) or which section is directly south of section 1 (12), and drawing the grid once from memory before the exam is the fastest way to get these right.

Reading a fractional description

Within a section, land is described by halves and quarters, and the description is written from the smallest piece to the largest.

Example: "the SE 1/4 of the NW 1/4 of Section 22, Township 46 South, Range 41 East."

To locate it, read from the end backward:

  1. Find Township 46 South, Range 41 East.
  2. Find Section 22 inside it.
  3. Take the northwest quarter of Section 22.
  4. Take the southeast quarter of that quarter.

To compute acreage, multiply the denominators of the fractions and divide 640 by the result:

  • 4 × 4 = 16; 640 ÷ 16 = 40 acres.

A few more for practice in reading, using the same section:

  • "The N 1/2 of the SW 1/4 of Section 22" — 2 × 4 = 8; 640 ÷ 8 = 80 acres.
  • "The W 1/2 of the NE 1/4 of the SE 1/4 of Section 22" — 2 × 4 × 4 = 32; 640 ÷ 32 = 20 acres.
  • "The NW 1/4 of Section 22" — 640 ÷ 4 = 160 acres.

The exam also asks for dimensions, not just acreage. A section is 5,280 feet on each side, so a quarter section is 2,640 feet by 2,640 feet, and a quarter of a quarter is 1,320 feet by 1,320 feet. The N 1/2 of a quarter section is 2,640 feet wide (east to west) and 1,320 feet deep (north to south). A buyer on Spanish Moss Trail who is told she is getting "the E 1/2 of the SE 1/4" can picture it as a strip 1,320 feet wide and 2,640 feet long, running north to south along the east edge of the southern half of the section. Fencing it, at a scenario cost per foot, is a perimeter question: 1,320 + 2,640 + 1,320 + 2,640 = 7,920 feet.

When a description uses the word "and" or a semicolon to join two separate pieces, compute each piece separately and add them. "The N 1/2 of the NE 1/4 and the SE 1/4 of the NE 1/4 of Section 22" is 80 acres plus 40 acres, which is 120 acres. Without the "and," the fractions would multiply into a smaller parcel.

The trap: the word "of" versus the word "and." "Of" means a piece inside the next piece, so you multiply denominators and the parcel gets smaller. "And" means two separate pieces, so you add them. The exam places both in one description to see which you notice.

Lot and block (recorded plat)

Most residential property in Florida is described this way. A developer subdividing land hires a surveyor to prepare a plat — a detailed map of the subdivision showing every lot, block, street, easement and common area, with exact dimensions, tied to the government survey or a metes-and-bounds boundary. The plat is approved by the local government and recorded in the county's official records, under Florida's platting law in Chapter 177 of the Florida Statutes.

Once the plat is recorded, any lot can be described by reference to it:

"Lot 14, Block C, of SILVER PALM ESTATES, according to the plat thereof recorded in Plat Book 112, Page 37, of the Public Records of Broward County, Florida."

That description incorporates everything on the plat — the lot's dimensions, its easements, its frontage — without repeating it. The four things it needs are the lot, the block, the subdivision name, and the plat book and page where the plat is recorded, plus the county.

Because the plat also shows easements and setback lines, a buyer can learn a lot about what can and cannot be built on a lot by looking at the plat.

The trap: the description that leaves out the recording reference — "Lot 14, Block C, Silver Palm Estates." In a county with two subdivisions of similar name, or after a replat, that description may not identify a single parcel. The recording reference is what makes lot and block a legal description.

Vertical descriptions and air rights

Land ownership runs both down below the surface and up into the air, within reasonable limits. A condominium unit on the 12th floor of a building on Magnolia Bend has to be described in three dimensions — not just the footprint on the ground, but how high above a fixed reference the unit's floor and ceiling are.

The reference point is a datum, an established elevation (generally measured from sea level) that surveyors use across the country. A benchmark is a permanent marker at a known elevation, tied to the datum, from which local elevations are measured.

In a condominium, the declaration and its attached survey describe each unit's horizontal and vertical boundaries, so the legal description of a unit is usually "Unit 1204 of [condominium name], according to the declaration of condominium recorded in Official Records Book __, Page __."

The trap: treating a condo unit's legal description like a lot-and-block description. A condo is described by reference to the recorded declaration, not a plat.

Surveys and computing area

A survey is a surveyor's map of the parcel showing its boundaries, the improvements on it, and any encroachments, easements or flood-zone information. Surveys are what reveal the neighbor's fence two feet over the line, which a title search will not. Lenders and title insurers commonly require one, and a buyer who skips it loses the protection.

Area calculations on the exam are mostly simple geometry:

  • Rectangle: length × width. A lot 100 feet by 150 feet is 15,000 square feet.
  • Triangle: base × height ÷ 2. A triangular corner lot with a 120-foot base and 90-foot height is 5,400 square feet.
  • Converting to acres: square feet ÷ 43,560. The 15,000-square-foot lot is about 0.34 acres.
  • Price per front foot: the side of the lot facing the street or water is the front footage, and it is listed first. A lot described as 80 × 125 has 80 feet of frontage and 125 feet of depth. At a scenario price of $2,500 per front foot, that lot is $200,000.

An irregular lot can be divided into rectangles and triangles, each calculated separately and then added.

The trap: front footage is always the first number. A lot on Osprey Ridge Road listed as "75 × 140" has 75 feet facing the road, not 140. A question that prices the lot by the front foot and expects you to multiply by depth is testing exactly that.

How to use this chapter

Draw the township grid with the 36 sections once from memory before opening the chapter, and keep the conversion list beside you while you work. Then read the fractional-description section right before the matching part of the chapter; the chapter's practice problems are where the "of" and "and" distinction actually sinks in.
