Study companion — Chapter 19: Planning, Zoning, and Environmental Hazards

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course, Chapter 19. Pre-reading: go through a section here, then open that part of the chapter, so the book reads as a second pass instead of a cold start.

What this chapter is. The government's power to decide how land may be used, and the physical hazards — flood, radon, lead, contamination, sinkholes — that can make a property worth less or cost more to own. The exam cares because both show up in nearly every transaction. A buyer who wants to put a home office in a garage, add a pool, or build on a beachfront lot runs into zoning, permits and coastal rules. A buyer of an older home in a flood zone runs into lead-paint, radon and flood disclosures. The exam tests the vocabulary of land-use control precisely, and it tests which disclosure is required in which deal.

Everything below is Florida law and Florida practice. Where a number is stated, it is certain. Where it is not, it says "under Florida law" — take the number from the textbook.

Where the power comes from

Land-use control is an exercise of the police power — the government's authority to regulate private property to protect public health, safety, morals and general welfare. It is one of the four government powers over land from earlier in the course (police power, eminent domain, taxation, escheat), and the one this whole chapter rests on.

The key difference from eminent domain: under police power the owner keeps the property and receives no compensation. Under eminent domain the government takes the property and must pay just compensation. A regulation that goes so far it leaves the owner no economically viable use can become a "taking" that requires compensation, which is the outer limit.

The trap: a county downzones a parcel on Slash Pine Road from commercial to residential, cutting its value by half. The owner demands payment. Zoning is police power; a value loss from regulation, by itself, is not compensable.

Florida's planning framework

Florida requires planning from the top down. The state sets goals; regional councils coordinate; every county and city must adopt a local comprehensive plan; and every local land-development regulation, including zoning, must be consistent with that plan.

The current state law is the Community Planning Act of 2011, which replaced the earlier growth-management act from 1985. It gave local governments more control and reduced the state's review role. The state land planning agency, now housed within FloridaCommerce (formerly the Department of Economic Opportunity, and before that the Department of Community Affairs), still reviews plan amendments.

A comprehensive plan is the long-range blueprint: where homes, businesses and industry should go, how roads and water lines will keep up, and what land should be protected. It is organized into elements — among them future land use, transportation, housing, public facilities (sewer, water, drainage, solid waste), conservation, recreation and open space, intergovernmental coordination, and capital improvements. Coastal local governments must also include a coastal management element. Take the full required list from the textbook.

Why Florida treats it this way: decades of fast growth outran roads, schools and water. The plan forces a city to say in advance what it intends, and the consistency requirement means a zoning decision that contradicts the plan can be challenged.

The trap: "zoning is the plan." Zoning is a tool that carries out the plan. The plan comes first; zoning must be consistent with it.

Concurrency

Concurrency means public facilities must be available at the same time as the development that will use them — "concurrent with the impacts of development." A 400-home subdivision on Spoonbill Point Drive cannot be approved if the sewer plant has no capacity left for it.

Under the 2011 act, concurrency remains mandatory for some facilities and became optional for others at the local government's choice; take the exact lists from the textbook. Developers often satisfy concurrency by paying impact fees — one-time charges that fund the new capacity their project requires.

The trap: confusing impact fees with special assessments. An impact fee is charged to the developer of new construction to pay for capacity the new project needs. A special assessment is charged to existing owners for an improvement that benefits their parcels.

Zoning: what it does

Zoning divides a city or county into districts and sets what may be built in each and how. Typical district types:

  • Residential — often subdivided by density (single-family, multifamily).
  • Commercial — retail, offices, services.
  • Industrial — manufacturing, warehousing.
  • Agricultural.
  • Mixed use and planned unit developments (PUDs) — a combination approved as one master plan, often trading higher density in one area for preserved open space in another.

Within each district, the ordinance sets use (what activity), bulk and density (lot size, height, floor area, units per acre), and setbacks (how far a building must stand from the lot lines).

Two zoning styles the chapter names:

  • Cumulative (or pyramid) zoning lets a "higher" use go into a "lower" district — a house could go in a commercial district, but not a factory in a residential one.
  • Exclusive zoning allows only the stated use in each district.

Buffer zones separate incompatible uses — a strip of park or a band of offices between a shopping center and a neighborhood.

Zoning is administered locally: a planning commission (or planning and zoning board) recommends; the county commission or city council, as the legislative body, adopts and amends the ordinance; and a board of adjustment (or zoning board of appeals) hears requests for variances and special exceptions.

Nonconforming use

A nonconforming use is a use that was legal when it began but no longer complies because the zoning changed afterward. It is "grandfathered" — allowed to continue.

Florida local ordinances usually limit it: the owner generally cannot expand it, may not be able to rebuild it if it is destroyed beyond a set percentage, and may lose it if the use is abandoned for a set period. Those thresholds come from each local ordinance; take any figures the course uses from the textbook.

Worked scene. A corner store at 1402 Loblolly Boulevard has operated since the 1970s. In 2010 the city rezoned the block to single-family residential. The store may keep operating as a nonconforming use. The owner may not add a second building for a laundromat. If the store burns down, the right to rebuild depends on the ordinance.

The trap: "nonconforming" versus "illegal." A nonconforming use was legal when it started. A use that never complied is simply a violation and is not grandfathered.

Variance, special exception, rezoning, spot zoning

Four ways to get something the zoning ordinance does not plainly allow. The exam asks which one fits the facts.

Variance — permission to deviate from a dimensional or physical requirement (a setback, a height limit) because applying it strictly would cause an unnecessary hardship unique to the property. The use itself stays allowed. A lot on Slash Pine Road is oddly shaped, and a house built to the ordinance's 25-foot rear setback (a scenario figure) would be unbuildably narrow; the owner seeks a variance to build at 18 feet. The hardship must come from the land, not from the owner's own choices, and a hardship the owner created is not grounds for a variance.

Special exception (also called a conditional use or special use permit) — a use that the ordinance already lists as allowed in the district if specific conditions are met and the board approves. A church or a day-care center in a residential district is the classic example.

Rezoning (zoning amendment) — changing the district classification of a parcel. This is a legislative act by the county commission or city council, and it must be consistent with the comprehensive plan; sometimes the plan has to be amended first.

Spot zoning — rezoning a single small parcel for a use that benefits its owner and is out of step with the surrounding area and the plan. It is generally treated as invalid.

The trap: variance versus special exception. A variance relaxes a rule (how). A special exception permits a use the ordinance already anticipates (what). A homeowner who wants to run a day care in a residential district is asking for a special exception, not a variance.

Subdivision regulations and building permits

Subdivision regulations control how raw land is split into lots: street widths, drainage, utility easements, lot sizes, and the dedication of land for public use. A developer files a plat — a map of the lots, blocks and streets — for approval and recording. Once recorded, lots can be described by reference to the plat.

Building permits are required before construction, major alteration, or demolition. Inspections follow at stages of the work, and when the building passes final inspection the local government issues a certificate of occupancy. Without it, the building may not legally be occupied.

Worked scene. A buyer looks at a house on Spoonbill Point Drive whose garage has been converted to a bedroom. The county's records show no permit for the conversion. The room may have to be removed, permitted after the fact, or brought up to code at the buyer's cost — and a lender or insurer may decline to count it. The listing licensee who knows the work was unpermitted and says nothing has a disclosure problem.

The trap: "unpermitted work is the buyer's risk alone." Under Florida's disclosure duty for residential sales, a seller and licensee who know about a fact that materially affects value and is not readily observable must disclose it.

The Florida Building Code

Florida adopted a single, statewide Florida Building Code in 2002, replacing a patchwork of local codes after Hurricane Andrew (1992) showed how badly some construction had performed. The code is maintained by the Florida Building Commission and updated on a regular cycle. Local governments enforce it; they may adopt local amendments only in limited ways.

The code includes strict wind-load and impact-protection requirements, and the most demanding standards apply in the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, which covers Miami-Dade and Broward counties. That is why window and door products in South Florida carry product approvals that other areas do not require.

The trap: "each Florida county writes its own building code." Florida has one statewide code; counties enforce it.

Deed restrictions versus zoning

Deed restrictions (restrictive covenants) are private limits written into deeds or a subdivision's recorded declaration — minimum house sizes, no boats in driveways, architectural approval. They are enforced by private parties, usually a homeowners' association or neighboring owners, through lawsuits. Zoning is public and enforced by government.

When the two conflict, the more restrictive one governs. If zoning allows a two-story house and the deed restrictions allow only one story, the owner builds one story.

The trap: "zoning overrides private restrictions." It does not. The stricter rule controls, whichever source it comes from.

Flood zones and the NFIP

The National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), run by FEMA, maps flood risk on Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRMs) and makes flood insurance available in participating communities. Standard homeowner's policies do not cover flood.

The map zones the exam uses:

  • Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) — land with a 1 percent annual chance of flooding (often called the "100-year flood plain"). Zones beginning with A (inland and riverine flooding) and V (coastal areas subject to wave action — "velocity") are SFHAs.
  • Zone X (and B and C on older maps) — moderate to minimal flood risk.

The base flood elevation (BFE) is the height the 1-percent-chance flood is expected to reach. An elevation certificate shows how high the building's lowest floor sits relative to the BFE, which drives the insurance premium.

Mandatory purchase: a property in an SFHA financed with a loan from a federally regulated or insured lender, or backed by a federal agency, must carry flood insurance. A new NFIP policy generally has a 30-day waiting period before it takes effect, with an exception when the policy is bought in connection with making, increasing or renewing a loan.

Florida also requires a flood disclosure to the buyer of residential property, given at or before the signing of the contract, stating whether the seller knows of past flood damage, has filed flood insurance claims, or received federal flood assistance. That requirement is in section 689.302 of the Florida Statutes, and Florida has a parallel requirement for landlords on longer residential leases. Take the exact form and timing from the textbook.

Worked scene. A buyer is closing in three weeks on a house at 88 Spoonbill Point Drive in Zone AE with an FHA loan. Flood insurance is mandatory. Because the policy is being bought in connection with the loan, the 30-day wait does not delay the closing. A buyer who instead paid cash and bought a policy three weeks before closing would have no flood coverage for the first 30 days.

The trap: "Zone X means no flood risk." It means lower mapped risk. A large share of flood claims nationally come from outside the SFHA. Insurance is not mandatory there; it can still be the right choice.

Coastal construction control line

Along Florida's sandy beaches, the state has drawn a coastal construction control line (CCCL). Seaward of that line, construction, major repairs, seawalls and even some excavation require a permit from the Florida Department of Environmental Protection, in addition to local permits. The line marks the zone where a major storm's waves and erosion are expected to reach, and the permit standards are tougher there: deeper foundations, elevation requirements, protection of dunes and sea-turtle nesting areas.

The CCCL is created under Chapter 161 of the Florida Statutes. Florida law also requires a disclosure to a buyer of property that lies partly or wholly seaward of the line; take its exact wording and timing from the textbook.

Worked scene. A buyer wants to tear down and rebuild an older beach cottage at 9 Spoonbill Point Drive, seaward of the CCCL. The county's building permit is not enough. The DEP permit may require a different footprint, a higher floor and pilings, and may take months. That is a value and timing fact the buyer needs before contract.

The trap: "the CCCL is a federal line." It is a Florida line, set and permitted by Florida DEP.

Radon

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas from the breakdown of uranium in soil and rock. It enters buildings through foundation cracks, and long-term exposure is a leading cause of lung cancer. It cannot be seen or smelled; only a test shows it.

Florida requires a specific radon notice, set out in section 404.056(5) of the Florida Statutes, to be given at the time of, or before, the contract for sale and purchase of any building or the execution of a rental agreement for any building. The required wording is:

"RADON GAS: Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that, when it has accumulated in a building in sufficient quantities, may present health risks to persons who are exposed to it over time. Levels of radon that exceed federal and state guidelines have been found in buildings in Florida. Additional information regarding radon and radon testing may be obtained from your county health department."

The law requires the notice, not a test. Florida's standard contract forms include it.

The trap: "radon disclosure applies only to homes." It applies to the sale or rental of any building — residential or commercial.

Lead-based paint

Lead-based paint was banned for residential use in the United States in 1978. Lead dust and chips are especially dangerous to young children and during pregnancy. The federal disclosure rule applies to the sale or lease of housing built before 1978:

  • The seller or landlord must disclose any known lead-based paint or hazards and provide any available reports.
  • The buyer or tenant must receive the EPA pamphlet Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home.
  • The contract or lease must include a Lead Warning Statement, signed by the parties.
  • A buyer must be offered a 10-day opportunity to conduct a risk assessment or inspection before becoming obligated, which the buyer may waive in writing.
  • The seller, landlord and the licensees involved must keep the signed disclosure for three years.

The licensee has a duty under this rule too: to make sure the seller or landlord knows their obligations and that the disclosures happen. A licensee who skips it shares the liability.

Worked scene. A 1962 house on Loblolly Boulevard is listed. The seller has never tested and knows of no lead. The seller still must give the pamphlet, the warning statement and the disclosure form stating there is no known lead, and the buyer still gets the 10-day inspection opportunity. "No known lead" is a disclosure, not an exemption.

The trap: the seller who says "I've never tested, so there's nothing to disclose." The rule is triggered by the 1978 construction date, not by what the seller knows. What the seller knows determines the content of the disclosure, not whether it is given.

Asbestos, mold, and contaminated land

Asbestos was used for decades in insulation, flooring, popcorn ceilings and roofing. It is dangerous mainly when disturbed and its fibers become airborne, which is why renovation and demolition are the risky moments. Removal is regulated and requires licensed contractors. Often the safer choice is encapsulation — sealing it in place.

Mold needs moisture, and Florida supplies plenty. It follows leaks, flooding, and poor air conditioning. There is no federal disclosure form for mold, but a known, material mold problem falls under the general duty to disclose facts that materially affect value.

Underground storage tanks (USTs) — old gas station or heating-oil tanks — can leak into soil and groundwater. Florida and federal law regulate them.

CERCLA (the Superfund law) makes owners of contaminated land potentially liable for cleanup costs, even if they did not cause the contamination. Liability can be joint and several and can fall on the current owner. A buyer of commercial property protects itself by conducting environmental due diligence before purchase, typically a Phase I environmental site assessment, which supports the "innocent landowner" defense. Brownfields are previously used sites with real or suspected contamination, and Florida has a program offering incentives to clean them up and redevelop them.

Worked scene. An investor wants to buy a former dry cleaner at 2300 Loblolly Boulevard. Dry-cleaning solvents are a common source of groundwater contamination. Without a Phase I before closing, the investor could become liable for a cleanup that costs more than the building.

The trap: "the polluter pays, so the new owner is safe." Under CERCLA the current owner can be liable regardless of who caused it.

Sinkholes and wetlands

Sinkholes form when limestone beneath the surface dissolves and the ground above collapses. Large parts of Florida sit on limestone. Florida law requires property insurers to cover catastrophic ground cover collapse — a sudden collapse that makes the home uninhabitable — while broader sinkhole coverage is generally offered as an option at additional cost. A known sinkhole history, or a past claim, is a material fact for disclosure.

Wetlands — marshes, swamps, mangroves — are protected by federal and state law. Filling or dredging them generally requires permits from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers under the federal Clean Water Act, and from Florida's DEP or a water management district. A lot that looks buildable may be largely unbuildable once wetlands are mapped.

Worked scene. A buyer finds a cheap two-acre lot on Slash Pine Road. A survey shows 1.5 acres are jurisdictional wetlands. The buildable area may be a quarter of what the buyer expected, and the price reflects that.

The trap: "standard homeowner's insurance covers all sinkhole damage in Florida." The required coverage is for catastrophic ground cover collapse. Damage short of that requires the optional coverage.

How to use this chapter

Read the zoning sections first and be able to tell variance, special exception, rezoning and spot zoning apart from a one-sentence fact pattern before opening the chapter. The environmental half is mostly disclosure rules — lead's 1978 date and 10 days, radon's "any building," flood insurance's 30-day wait.
