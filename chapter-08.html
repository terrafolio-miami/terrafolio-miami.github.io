Chapter 8 — Property Rights: Estates and Tenancies; Condominiums, Cooperatives, Community Development Districts, Homeowner Associations, and Time-Sharing

What this chapter is. The vocabulary of ownership: what exactly a person owns when they "own" property, for how long, with whom, and under what kind of shared arrangement. Every later chapter assumes you can name the interest being transferred. The exam tests this chapter with definitions that sit one word apart (determinable versus condition subsequent, remainder versus reversion, joint tenancy versus tenancy by the entireties), and with Florida rules that differ from the general national picture, especially homestead, survivorship, and the condominium and timeshare cancellation rights.

Where a number below is stated, it has been checked against the current Florida Statutes. Where it says "under Florida law" without a number, take the number from the textbook.

Real property, personal property and fixtures

Real property is the land, everything permanently attached to it, and the rights that go with owning it. Personal property (personalty) is everything movable: furniture, a car, a boat on a trailer. The line between them matters because a sale of real property carries everything that counts as real property unless the contract says otherwise, and a buyer and seller who disagree about the chandelier are really disagreeing about this line.

A fixture is an item that started as personal property and became real property by being attached. The tests the exam uses are the method of attachment (bolted, plumbed, wired in), adaptation to the property (custom-cut blinds for odd windows), the intention of the person who attached it, and any agreement between the parties. Agreement beats everything: if the contract says the wall-mounted television stays or goes, that settles it.

Trade fixtures are the exception. Equipment a commercial tenant installs for its business — the pizza oven, the salon chairs bolted to the floor — remains the tenant's personal property and can be removed before the lease ends, as long as the tenant repairs the damage.

The trap: a seller on Mango Street removes the ceiling fans and the built-in bookcase before closing, saying "they're mine, I bought them." They were hers. The moment they were wired and built in, they became part of the house, and unless the contract excluded them, they belonged to the buyer at closing. The other trap is a restaurant tenant's walk-in cooler — a trade fixture, and the landlord does not get to keep it.

The bundle of rights

Ownership is described as a bundle of separate rights: to possess, to use, to enjoy, to exclude others, and to dispose of the property (sell, give, lease, mortgage, or leave it by will). An owner can hand out individual sticks from the bundle — a lease gives away possession for a time, an easement gives away a limited use — and still own the property.

Government keeps certain rights over all private property — police power, eminent domain, taxation, escheat — covered in the next chapter.

The trap: a landlord who leases out a duplex on Tamarind Avenue has not "given up ownership." The landlord kept the bundle minus possession, which comes back when the lease ends.

Water rights

Florida has a lot of shoreline, so the exam spends time here. Riparian rights belong to owners of land along a river or stream (flowing water). Littoral rights belong to owners along a lake, sea or ocean (still or tidal water). Both include reasonable use of the water and access to it.

In Florida, land below the mean high-water line of navigable waters is generally owned by the state and held in trust for the public. That is why a beachfront owner owns down to that line, not into the ocean, and why the public can use the wet sand.

The trap: calling a beachfront owner's rights "riparian." Ocean frontage is littoral. The exam will give both words and expect the one that matches the type of water.

Freehold estates: fee simple

An estate is the size and duration of an interest in land. Freehold estates last for an indefinite period; nonfreehold estates (leases) are covered below.

Fee simple absolute is the largest estate the law recognizes: unlimited in time, freely transferable, inheritable, with no conditions attached. When people say they "own" a house, this is almost always what they own.

Fee simple defeasible is a fee simple that can be lost if a condition is broken. There are two forms, and the exam tests the difference:

  • Fee simple determinable ends automatically if the condition is broken. The language is about duration: "so long as," "while," "during." Breaking the condition ends the estate on its own, and the property reverts to the grantor or heirs (the possibility of reverter).
  • Fee simple subject to a condition subsequent does not end automatically. The language is conditional: "on the condition that," "provided that," "but if." If the condition is broken, the grantor or heirs have a right of re-entry and must take action to get the property back.

The trap: a donor deeds a lot on Seagrape Circle to a church "so long as it is used for worship." The church turns it into a parking lot. Ownership has already gone back to the donor's heirs, without a lawsuit, because "so long as" made it determinable. Change the words to "on the condition that," and the heirs have to act.

Life estates

A life estate lasts for the life of a named person. Usually that person is the life tenant, but a life estate pur autre vie is measured by someone else's life: an estate to Rosa for the life of her father ends when her father dies, not when Rosa does.

When the life estate ends, the property goes somewhere. If it goes back to the original grantor, that future interest is a reversion. If it goes to a third person named in the deed, it is a remainder, held by a remainderman.

The life tenant can live in the property, rent it out, or sell the life estate — but can only sell what they have, which is an interest that ends when the measuring life ends. The life tenant also has a duty not to damage the property's value to the harm of the remainderman (waste), and generally pays the taxes and ordinary upkeep.

Florida abolished dower and curtesy, the old common-law life estates a surviving spouse took in the other's land. Protection for a surviving spouse in Florida now comes through homestead rules and the probate code's elective share instead.

The trap: a life tenant on Cypress Hollow Road leases the farm for ten years, then dies in year three. The lease ends with the life estate. A tenant can't hold more than the landlord had. The other trap is reversion versus remainder: "back to the grantor" is reversion; "on to someone else" is remainder.

Nonfreehold estates (leaseholds)

These are tenancies — the right to possess property owned by someone else, for a time.

  • Estate for years: a definite beginning and a definite end. It does not have to be a year; a six-month lease with fixed dates is an estate for years. It ends automatically on its end date with no notice required.
  • Periodic tenancy (estate from year to year, month to month): renews automatically for successive periods until one side gives proper notice. The rent period usually sets the period of the tenancy.
  • Tenancy at will: no fixed term, ends when either side chooses, with whatever notice the law requires. Under Florida law the notice depends on the rent period; take the specifics from the book.
  • Tenancy at sufferance: a tenant who stays after the right to possession has ended, without the landlord's consent. This is the weakest interest: the occupant came in lawfully, so is not treated as a trespasser at first, but has no right to stay and can be removed.

Death of the landlord or tenant does not end an estate for years or a periodic tenancy; the lease binds the estate. Death does end a tenancy at will.

The trap: the word "years." A lease on Mango Street running from February 1 to July 31 is an estate for years even though it lasts six months. The test is fixed dates, not length.

Forms of ownership: severalty and co-ownership

Severalty means one owner alone — "severed" from others. A corporation that owns a building owns it in severalty.

Tenancy in common is the default for co-owners who are not married to each other. Each owns an undivided interest (they share the whole property; neither owns a particular room), the shares can be unequal, each can sell or leave their share by will without the others' consent, and there is no right of survivorship. When a tenant in common dies, the share goes to that person's heirs or devisees, not to the co-owners.

Joint tenancy includes the right of survivorship: when one joint tenant dies, the survivors take that share automatically, outside probate. The classic requirements are the four unities: time, title, interest and possession — the joint tenants acquired their interests at the same time, by the same deed, in equal shares, with equal rights to possess the whole. A joint tenant who sells their share breaks the unities for that share; the buyer becomes a tenant in common with the rest.

Florida's twist: Florida does not presume survivorship. Under Florida Statutes section 689.15, a conveyance to two or more people creates a tenancy in common unless the instrument expressly provides for the right of survivorship (tenancy by the entireties is the exception). A deed that just says "to Alma and Teo as joint tenants" may not be enough. The deed has to say "with full rights of survivorship" or equivalent words.

Tenancy by the entireties is available only to a married couple. The couple holds the property as a single unit; each spouse owns the whole, there is survivorship, neither spouse can sell or mortgage it alone, and a creditor of only one spouse generally cannot reach it. In Florida, a deed to a married couple is presumed to create a tenancy by the entireties unless it says otherwise. Divorce converts it to a tenancy in common.

The trap: two unmarried partners on Tamarind Avenue buy a condo, the deed names them both and says nothing more, and one dies. In Florida that is a tenancy in common; the survivor does not automatically take the other's half. Married, with the same deed, they would have been tenants by the entireties and the survivor would own it all.

Ownership by business entities and trusts

Property can be owned by a corporation (in severalty), a partnership, an LLC, or through a trust. Florida recognizes the land trust, in which a trustee holds legal title and the beneficiaries hold the rights to use and control the property and to its income, which the law treats as personal property. The trust's main draws are privacy (the public record shows the trustee's name, not the beneficiaries') and ease of transferring interests.

The trap: assuming a deed to a trustee on Seagrape Circle means the trustee personally owns the house. The trustee holds title for the beneficiaries; the trust document says who actually controls it.

Florida homestead

Florida's homestead rules are in Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution and related statutes, and they do three separate things. The exam keeps them separate.

1. Protection from forced sale. A Florida resident's homestead cannot be taken by most creditors to satisfy debts. The protected size is up to one-half acre of contiguous land within a municipality, or up to 160 contiguous acres outside a municipality. The protection does not apply against property taxes and assessments on the home, a mortgage on the home, or debts for work and materials used to improve the home.

2. Restrictions on transfer. If the owner is married, the homestead cannot be sold or mortgaged without the spouse joining in the deed or mortgage, even if the spouse is not on title. If the owner dies survived by a spouse or a minor child, the homestead cannot be freely left by will to someone else; Florida law dictates who takes it.

3. Property tax exemption. A Florida resident who owns and lives in the home as a permanent residence on January 1 can apply for the homestead exemption with the county property appraiser. The application deadline is March 1. The basic exemption is $25,000 off the assessed value, and applies to all property taxes including school taxes. There is an additional exemption that applies to assessed value above a higher band and not to school taxes; its amount is now adjusted for inflation, and a constitutional amendment on the November 2026 ballot would change the non-school exemption substantially if voters approve it. Take the current figures from the textbook; the exam tests whatever the book uses.

The Save Our Homes cap limits how much the assessed value of a homestead can rise each year: 3 percent or the change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is lower. When the home is sold, the assessment resets to market value for the new owner, though Florida allows an owner to carry some accumulated benefit to a new homestead (portability); take the details from the book.

The trap: blending the three. The half-acre and 160-acre limits are about creditor protection, not the tax exemption. The $25,000 is tax, not creditor protection. And the spouse-signature rule applies even when only one spouse is on title — a husband on Cypress Hollow Road who owns the homestead in his own name before marriage still needs his wife to join the deed to sell it.

Condominiums (Chapter 718)

A condominium is a form of ownership, not a type of building. Each owner holds fee simple title to a unit plus an undivided interest in the common elements — the land, roof, hallways, pool, elevators. Each unit is bought, sold, mortgaged and taxed on its own.

A condominium is created by recording a declaration of condominium in the county records. The declaration describes the units, the common elements, each unit's share of them, and the rules. The condominium association — every unit owner is a member — runs the property through a board and collects assessments.

Limited common elements are common elements reserved for one or a few units: an assigned parking space, a unit's balcony.

Buyer protection is where the numbers live:

  • Buying from the developer. The buyer must receive the required documents (the declaration, bylaws, budget, prospectus and others) and can cancel the contract within 15 days after the later of signing the contract or receiving all required documents.
  • Buying a resale from an owner. The buyer is entitled to the association's governing documents and other required information. Under section 718.503 as amended effective July 1, 2025, the buyer can cancel within 7 days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays, after the later of signing or receiving the documents. Earlier versions of the statute gave three days, and some printings of the textbook may still say three; the current statute says seven.

The trap: the two periods. 15 days for a developer sale, a shorter business-day period for a resale. And the other trap: a condo owner on Mango Street owns her unit, not "a share in the building." That is the co-op.

Cooperatives (Chapter 719)

In a cooperative, a corporation owns the building. The residents own shares of stock in the corporation and hold a proprietary lease to their unit. Because what they own is stock plus a lease, a co-op interest has historically been treated as personal property, not real property, though Florida law treats co-op interests as real property for some purposes, including homestead; take the specifics from the book.

The corporation pays one property tax bill and, often, one underlying mortgage on the whole building, and passes those costs to the shareholders. If enough shareholders stop paying, the rest of them can be left covering the shortfall. Co-ops can usually control who buys in, because a sale is a transfer of shares that the board can approve or deny.

The trap: confusing a co-op with a condo. Ownership of a unit with a deed and its own tax bill is a condo. Shares plus a lease and a shared building mortgage is a co-op.

Community Development Districts (Chapter 190)

A CDD is a special-purpose local government created under Chapter 190 to finance and maintain infrastructure for a new community — roads, water and sewer, drainage, sometimes clubhouses and landscaping. The CDD issues bonds to pay for the infrastructure up front, and property owners repay them through assessments that appear on the property tax bill, often for many years, along with ongoing maintenance assessments.

Because those assessments are real, recurring and easy to miss, Florida requires a specific disclosure in the initial contract for the sale of residential property inside a CDD, telling the buyer that the property is subject to the district's taxes and assessments. Take the exact wording requirement from the book.

A CDD is a government, not a private association. Its board is elected, its meetings are public, and it does not enforce architectural rules about paint colors and fences. That is usually an HOA's job, and many CDD communities have both.

The trap: a buyer in a new subdivision off Seagrape Circle sees a tax bill well above what the purchase price would suggest. The extra is the CDD assessment, and it is a lien on the property just like property taxes. The other trap is calling a CDD an HOA: one is a unit of local government under Chapter 190, the other a private corporation under Chapter 720.

Homeowners' associations (Chapter 720)

An HOA governs a community of separately owned homes — usually single-family houses or townhouses — where each owner owns their own lot and house, and the association owns and maintains common areas (entrance, pool, retention ponds) and enforces deed restrictions. Membership is mandatory for owners, and the association can collect assessments and record liens for unpaid ones.

The key Florida rule for licensees is the disclosure summary under section 720.401. A prospective buyer of a home in a community with a mandatory HOA must be given a disclosure summary, in the form the statute prescribes, before signing the contract. If the contract does not include or reference it, the buyer may void the contract within 3 days after receiving the disclosure summary or before closing, whichever comes first. The disclosure tells the buyer that membership is mandatory, that assessments exist and can change, that failure to pay can lead to a lien and foreclosure, and that the restrictive covenants can be amended.

The trap: assuming an HOA is like a condo association. In an HOA the owner owns the lot and house outright; there is no undivided share in the building. And the exam's version of the disclosure question: the buyer signs, then gets the summary, then tries to cancel two weeks later after closing. Too late — the right ends at closing.

Time-sharing (Chapter 721)

A timeshare divides use of a property — usually a resort unit — into time periods sold to different buyers. Florida law recognizes two kinds:

  • A timeshare estate is an ownership interest in real property — a deeded share, coupled with a right to use a unit during a set time. It is real property.
  • A timeshare license (a right-to-use) is a contractual right to occupy a unit for a period, without an ownership interest. It is personal property.

The developer must give the buyer a public offering statement and other required documents. The buyer has a cancellation right that cannot be waived: under section 721.10, the buyer may cancel until midnight of the 10th calendar day after the later of signing the contract or receiving the last of the required documents. The contract must state this right conspicuously.

Selling timeshares for compensation is real estate brokerage in Florida, so the sales staff must be licensed; take the specifics on exemptions and resale from the book.

The trap: the count. Ten calendar days for a timeshare — not business days, and not the condo's 15. A buyer who signs at a resort on Tamarind Avenue on a Saturday and receives all documents that day can cancel until midnight on the Tuesday ten days later, both weekends included.

How to use this chapter

Read the estates and co-ownership sections before opening the chapter; those are the definitions the rest of the book leans on. Then read homestead and the community-ownership sections right before those parts of the chapter, and write the cancellation periods (15, the resale period, 3, 10) on one card side by side — the exam will swap them.
