Study companion — Chapters 5, 6 and 7

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course. Read a section, then open that part of the chapter — it should feel like a re-read, not a cold start.

Everything below is Florida law and Florida practice — Chapter 475 of the Florida Statutes and the FREC rules. Where I am sure of a number, it is stated. Where I am not, it says "under Florida law" and you should take the number from the textbook, not from here. The exam tests edges and deadlines, so the numbers in the book are the ones that count.

Chapter 5 — Real Estate Brokerage Activities and Procedures

What this chapter is. The mechanics of running a brokerage legally: where you work, what you put on the sign and in the ads, and above all what happens to other people's money. The exam loves this chapter because almost every rule has a number of days attached to it, and the wrong number is always one of the answer choices.

The office and the sign

Every active broker has to maintain an office and register it with the state. It can be in a residence if local zoning allows it, but it has to exist and it has to be registered. At the entrance there has to be a sign that a person can read from outside, carrying the broker's name, the brokerage's trade name if it uses one, and words that identify it as a real estate business — "Licensed Real Estate Broker" or the standard abbreviation.

Why: the public has to be able to find the person who is actually accountable. A sales associate is never that person, which is why a sales associate may not maintain an office of their own. Your name can appear on the sign, but only underneath the broker's.

The trap: a broker who runs the business from a spare bedroom on Sawgrass Court, sees clients only at coffee shops, and has no sign. Working from home is fine; having no registered office with no sign is the violation. The other version is the associate who rents a storefront, calls it a "desk," and puts their own name over the door.

Branch offices

Any additional location where the brokerage does business is a branch office, and each one must be registered separately, with its own fee and its own sign. The one exception the exam likes: a temporary shelter on a subdivision being sold — a trailer on the site for shade and signing — is not a branch office if it is used only for that subdivision's sales and nothing else is conducted from it.

The trap: a broker opens a second location across town, staffs it with two associates, and never registers it because "it's the same company." Same company is exactly why it is a branch.

Advertising

Every ad the brokerage or any of its associates runs has to include the registered name of the brokerage in a way a reader can actually see. An ad that doesn't is a blind ad, and blind ads are prohibited. On the internet the rule follows every page and every point of contact — a listing page, a social post, a profile.

A sales associate's own name may appear, and so may a team name, but only alongside the brokerage name, never instead of it. Under Florida law team names also have limits on what they can contain, so they do not sound like a brokerage in their own right; take the specifics from the book.

The trap: a yard-sign rider on Bayshore Lane that says "The Rivera Group — call Daniel" with a cell number and nothing else. Personal branding is allowed; a brand standing alone is a blind ad, and the broker is responsible for it even if they never saw it.

Escrow — the deadlines

This is the heart of the chapter. Memorize these three, in this order:

A sales associate who receives a deposit must deliver it to the broker by the end of the next business day. The broker must place it in the escrow account by the end of the third business day after the brokerage received it. "Received" means the day the associate got it, not the day it reached the broker's desk.

The deposit goes into a separate escrow account at a bank, savings association, credit union or trust company located in Florida. The broker can choose instead not to hold it at all and let a Florida title company with trust powers or a Florida attorney hold it — but then the broker has to confirm in writing, within the time Florida law sets, that the money actually arrived, and pass that confirmation to the other side's broker.

Why: the money is the public's, not the brokerage's. Every deadline exists so that at no moment is a deposit sitting in someone's drawer or someone's personal account.

The trap: "business day." A deposit handed over Friday evening is received Friday; the associate's deadline is Monday, the broker's deadline is Wednesday. The exam will offer you the calendar-day answer and hope you take it.

Commingling and conversion

Commingling is mixing escrow money with the brokerage's own money. Conversion is using escrow money as if it were your own. Commingling is the lesser offense and still disciplinable; conversion is theft with a nicer name.

Florida allows a small cushion of the broker's own money in the escrow account to cover bank charges: up to $1,000 in a sales escrow account, up to $5,000 in a property-management escrow account. Those are legal thresholds, not scenario amounts.

The trap: a broker short on payroll "borrows" $4,000 from escrow on Tuesday and puts it back Friday. Intent to repay is not a defense. It was conversion the moment it left.

Monthly reconciliation

Once a month the broker must prepare a written reconciliation of every escrow account — the bank balance, the outstanding items, the total of all the individual deposits — then sign it and date it, and keep it. Shortages and overages have to be explained and corrected.

The trap: the bank statement is not the reconciliation. A broker who files the statements and never writes the comparison has not reconciled anything.

Conflicting demands

When buyer and seller both claim the deposit, or the broker has a good-faith doubt about who is entitled to it, the broker may not decide. The broker must notify FREC in writing within 15 business days of the last demand, and then within 30 business days start one of the four authorized settlement procedures: mediation (if both parties agree, and it has to finish within 90 days), arbitration (if both agree), litigation (which includes interpleader, where the broker hands the money to the court and lets a judge decide), or an escrow disbursement order from FREC.

There are a few situations where the notice to FREC is not required, mainly contracts that fall through on a financing condition and certain government-backed sales; take the exact list from the book.

The trap: sitting on it. A contested deposit on Heron Bay Drive left in the account for a year with no notice and no procedure is the violation, regardless of who deserved the money. The other trap is the broker who follows the EDO, gets sued anyway, and is then protected — FREC backs a broker who obeyed its order.

Compensation, rental lists, records

Only the employing broker can pay a sales associate for brokerage work. A grateful seller's thank-you check and a cooperating broker's direct payment are both violations.

If a brokerage sells rental lists, Florida requires a written contract and a refund rule: a customer who does not find a rental within 30 days gets 75 percent of the fee back on request, and a customer who finds the list was inaccurate gets 100 percent.

Brokerage records — deposits, contracts, reconciliations — are kept for five years, or longer if litigation is pending.

Chapter 6 — Violations of License Law, Penalties and Procedures

What this chapter is. What happens when a licensee breaks the rules in Chapter 5: the complaint process step by step, the menu of penalties, and the Real Estate Recovery Fund. The exam tests the order of the steps and the dollar and year limits. This is shorter than Chapter 5 and more memorizable.

The complaint process, in order

  1. Complaint filed with the Department of Business and Professional Regulation. Anyone can file one; DBPR can also start one itself. The complaint has to be legally sufficient — meaning that if what it alleges is true, it would be a violation. "He was rude to me" is not legally sufficient. "He kept my deposit" is.
  2. Investigation by DBPR. The licensee is told, except in cases where telling them would compromise the investigation.
  3. Probable cause panel. Usually two FREC members, at least one of whom is a current member, decide whether there is enough to proceed. If not, the case is dismissed. If so —
  4. Formal complaint is filed against the licensee. Now the licensee chooses:
  5. Informal hearing before FREC, if the licensee does not dispute the facts (a stipulation — essentially a plea agreement); or a formal hearing before an administrative law judge at the Division of Administrative Hearings, if the facts are disputed. The judge issues a recommended order.
  6. Final order by FREC. The Commission can accept, reject or modify the recommended penalty — but it cannot change the judge's findings of fact without a good reason on the record.
  7. Appeal to a District Court of Appeal, within 30 days of the final order. Filing an appeal does not by itself stop the penalty; the licensee has to ask for a stay.

One shortcut the exam tests: if a licensee's conduct is an immediate danger to the public, the DBPR Secretary can issue a summary (emergency) suspension before any of this plays out.

The trap: swapping the probable cause panel and the hearing. Probable cause comes before the formal complaint, not after. And the administrative law judge recommends; FREC decides.

The penalties FREC can impose

Denial of a license or renewal. Reprimand. Probation. Fine of up to $5,000 per count. Suspension up to 10 years. Revocation, which is permanent — except that revocation for a purely clerical mistake (an error in the application, for instance) is "without prejudice" and the person can reapply.

For minor first-time violations there are two lighter tools: a notice of noncompliance (a warning with a deadline to fix it) and a citation (a fixed fine for a listed minor offense). A licensee who gets a citation and does nothing for 30 days has accepted it.

The trap: the exam offers "a $5,000 fine and 10 years' suspension" as the maximum per violation; it also offers "revocation" as something FREC can do only through a court. FREC revokes on its own authority. Courts are for appeal.

Criminal side

License law violations are mostly second-degree misdemeanors. The big one that is worse: operating without a license is a third-degree felony. The exam will also ask about failing to give the required brokerage relationship disclosures, which is a license-law violation, not a crime by itself.

The words that are violations

The chapter has a vocabulary list and the exam uses it precisely:

  • Fraud: intentional deception to get someone to act. Misrepresentation: a false statement of fact, even an innocent one, that someone relied on. Concealment: hiding something you had a duty to disclose. These are three separate things.
  • Culpable negligence: not fraud, but carelessness so bad it is reckless.
  • Moral turpitude: conduct that is base or depraved — the exam's example is usually a crime involving dishonesty, not a traffic offense.
  • Failure to account for funds, and failure to deliver a deposit, are their own listed offenses.
  • A license that is void never existed or has been revoked; one that is ineffective exists but cannot be used (an associate whose broker's license is suspended is ineffective, not void); one that is involuntarily inactive lapsed because the licensee did not renew.

The trap: a sales associate whose broker is suspended. The associate did nothing wrong, keeps the license, but cannot work until they move to a new broker. The license is not revoked and not void.

The Real Estate Recovery Fund

A fund, paid into by licensees through their fees, that reimburses a member of the public who was harmed by a licensee's act and cannot collect from the licensee. The consumer first has to win a judgment (or arbitration award) and show it cannot be collected. The fund pays up to $50,000 per transaction, and up to $150,000 total against any one licensee no matter how many claims.

When the fund pays on a licensee's behalf, that licensee's license is automatically suspended until the fund is repaid in full with interest.

Who cannot collect: the licensee themselves, their spouse, or anyone who was acting as a licensee in the deal. A broker who is cheated by their own associate cannot claim from the fund — the fund is for the public.

The trap: the two numbers. $50,000 is per transaction. $150,000 is the lifetime cap per licensee. The exam swaps them.

Chapter 7 — Federal and State Laws Pertaining to Real Estate

What this chapter is. The laws from outside Chapter 475 that still govern what a licensee may do: fair housing above all, then disability access, landlord-tenant rules, and a handful of marketing and land-sale statutes. Fair housing is the part that matters most on the exam and in practice, and it is also the part people are most confident about and most often get wrong.

Fair housing — the two laws

Two federal laws, and the exam wants you to know which is which.

The Civil Rights Act of 1866 prohibits discrimination based on race in any property transaction, anywhere, with no exemptions at all. It sat mostly unused for a century until the Supreme Court confirmed in 1968 (Jones v. Mayer) that it means what it says.

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, amended in 1988) covers housing and prohibits discrimination based on seven protected classes: race, color, religion, national origin, sex, familial status, and handicap (disability). Florida's own Fair Housing Act mirrors the federal list. Local ordinances may add more classes; the federal list is the exam's list.

Familial status means households with children under 18, pregnant women, and people in the process of getting custody. Handicap includes physical and mental impairments and requires the landlord to allow reasonable modifications at the tenant's expense and to make reasonable accommodations in rules.

The exemptions, and why race is never one

The Fair Housing Act has exemptions. A single-family home sold or rented by an owner who owns no more than three such homes, without using a broker and without discriminatory advertising. An owner-occupied building of four units or fewer (the "Mrs. Murphy" exemption). Religious organizations and private clubs giving preference to their own members for non-commercial housing. Housing for older persons that meets the statutory tests.

None of these exemptions apply to race, because the 1866 Act has no exemptions. That is the single most-tested idea in the chapter. And none of them apply to a licensee: the moment a broker is involved, the owner's exemption is gone.

The prohibited practices by name

  • Steering: guiding buyers toward or away from neighborhoods based on a protected class. "You'd be more comfortable on the east side."
  • Blockbusting: inducing owners to sell by suggesting a protected class is moving in and values will fall.
  • Redlining: refusing to lend or insure in an area because of the people who live there.
  • Discriminatory advertising: any ad that states or implies a preference. The exam uses "ideal for a single professional" and "no children" as examples.
  • Refusing to sell, rent, or negotiate; setting different terms; falsely saying a property is unavailable.

The trap: steering by good intentions. A licensee on Coral Way who shows a family with three children only the houses near the elementary school, because "that's what they'll want," has steered on familial status. Helpful is not a defense. Let the buyer tell you what they want.

Enforcement

A complaint goes to HUD within one year, or to federal court as a civil action within two years. HUD investigates and tries conciliation; it can refer to an administrative law judge, and the Justice Department can sue where there is a pattern. Florida complaints can also go to the Florida Commission on Human Relations. Brokers are expected to display the fair housing poster; failing to display it is not a violation in itself but is treated as evidence if a complaint is filed.

The Americans with Disabilities Act

A different law with a different scope. The ADA is not about housing; it is about public accommodations and commercial facilities — a brokerage office, a store, a hotel. Barriers have to be removed where that is "readily achievable," and new construction and alterations have to be accessible. For a licensee the exam angle is: your own office is a public accommodation, and a commercial property you sell or lease carries ADA obligations the buyer or tenant needs to know about.

The trap: confusing ADA with the handicap provisions of fair housing. Housing is fair housing. Businesses are ADA.

Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act

Chapter 83 of the Florida Statutes, Part II, for residential tenancies. A licensee who manages property lives in this one. The exam deadlines:

  • Security deposits. The landlord holds the deposit in a Florida account (separate, or posts a bond), and tells the tenant in writing where it is held and whether it earns interest. After the tenant moves out the landlord has 15 days to return the deposit if there is no claim against it, or 30 days to send written notice of the intent to keep some or all of it and why. The tenant then has 15 days to object in writing. A landlord who misses the 30-day notice forfeits the right to keep any of it.
  • Nonpayment of rent: a three-day notice (not counting weekends and legal holidays) to pay or vacate before eviction can start.
  • Other lease violations: a seven-day notice to cure, or seven days to vacate if the violation is one that can't be cured or repeats.
  • Landlord's access: reasonable notice for repairs, with the statute giving a specific minimum; take the number from the book.

The trap: the security-deposit sequence. 15 to return, 30 to claim, 15 to object. The exam will give you "30 days to return" and hope you take it.

Land sales, telemarketing and the rest

Interstate Land Sales Full Disclosure Act (federal): a developer selling or leasing 25 or more unimproved lots across state lines must register with the federal government and give each buyer a property report before signing. The buyer has a rescission period after receiving it; take the number of days from the book. Florida has a parallel state law for subdivided land sold within Florida.

Telephone and email marketing. The federal Do-Not-Call rules and Florida's own telemarketing law restrict cold calls to numbers on the registry, with narrow exceptions for an existing business relationship. The federal CAN-SPAM Act governs commercial email: a working opt-out, an accurate "from," a physical address. The Junk Fax rule does the same for faxes. The exam angle is simple: a licensee prospecting by phone has to check the registry, and "I was calling about a listing, not selling" is not an exception.

The trap: the expired-listing call. A licensee calls the owner of a just-expired listing on Palmetto Lane whose number is on the Do-Not-Call registry. The old listing was with a different brokerage, so there is no existing business relationship with this licensee. That call is a violation.
