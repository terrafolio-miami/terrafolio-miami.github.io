Study companion — Chapter 18: Taxes Affecting Real Estate

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course, Chapter 18. Pre-reading: go through a section here, then open that part of the chapter, so the book reads as a second pass instead of a cold start.

What this chapter is. Two kinds of tax: Florida's property tax, which every owner pays every year, and the federal income tax rules that apply when a home or investment property is sold. Florida has no state personal income tax, so property tax is the state-level tax that matters to homeowners — and Florida's version has its own vocabulary, its own exemptions and its own calendar. The exam tests the computation step by step, the homestead rules, the payment calendar, and what happens when taxes go unpaid. The federal half is lighter: basis, gain, the home-sale exclusion, depreciation, and the like-kind exchange.

Everything below is Florida law and Florida practice. Where a number is stated, it is certain. Where it is not, it says "under Florida law" — take the number from the textbook.

Who does what

Three county officials, each with one job. The exam asks which one does which.

  • Property appraiser — sets the value of every parcel and administers exemptions. Does not set tax rates and does not collect anything.
  • Taxing authorities — the county commission, the city, the school board, and special districts (water management, fire, hospital, mosquito control). Each sets its own millage rate through a budget process.
  • Tax collector — sends the bills, collects the money, and runs the tax certificate sale when bills go unpaid.

There is also the value adjustment board (VAB), which hears owners' appeals of value and exemption decisions.

The trap: blaming the property appraiser for a higher tax bill. The appraiser sets value. The rate is set by the taxing authorities. A homeowner on Satinwood Terrace whose value stayed flat but whose tax went up should look at the millage, not the appraiser.

Ad valorem tax and the January 1 date

"Ad valorem" means according to value. Florida property tax is based on the property's value as of January 1 of the tax year. The tax year is the calendar year, January 1 through December 31.

Property tax is paid in arrears: the tax for a given year is billed in the fall of that year (bills go out around November) and paid by the following spring. And the tax becomes a lien on the property as of January 1 — a lien that is superior to other liens, including a mortgage recorded before it.

The trap: "taxes are paid in advance." In Florida they are paid in arrears. This matters at closing in the proration chapter: the seller has used the property for part of the year but has not yet paid that year's tax, so the seller owes the buyer a credit.

Just value, assessed value, taxable value

Three values, in order. Getting them in the right order is half of every tax problem.

Just value is market value — what the property would sell for on January 1, as determined by the property appraiser. Florida's constitution uses the term "just value."

Assessed value is just value after any assessment limitation, like the Save Our Homes cap (below). For a property with no cap, assessed value equals just value. Assessed value can never be higher than just value.

Taxable value is assessed value minus exemptions. This is the number the tax rate is applied to.

The order: just value → (cap) → assessed value → (exemptions) → taxable value → × millage → tax.

Millage

Florida tax rates are stated in mills. One mill is one-tenth of a cent, which works out to $1 of tax per $1,000 of taxable value.

Three ways to convert, all the same:

  • Mills ÷ 1,000 = a decimal rate. 18 mills = 0.018.
  • Taxable value ÷ 1,000 × mills = tax.
  • Taxable value × mills ÷ 1,000 = tax.

Worked example. A property at 912 Satinwood Terrace has a taxable value of $200,000. The total millage is 18 mills.

  • $200,000 ÷ 1,000 = 200 thousands.
  • 200 × 18 = $3,600.

Check: $200,000 × 0.018 = $3,600.

The Florida constitution caps how many mills counties, cities and school boards may levy; take the cap figures from the textbook.

The trap: treating a mill as one percent. One mill is one-tenth of one percent. A 20-mill rate is 2 percent.

The homestead exemption

Florida's constitution gives a homestead exemption to a person who holds legal or equitable title to Florida real property and makes it their permanent residence as of January 1. The owner applies to the property appraiser, and the application is due by March 1 of the year the exemption is to begin. Once granted, it renews automatically as long as the owner keeps qualifying.

The exemption has two parts:

  • The first $25,000 of assessed value is exempt from all property taxes, including school taxes.
  • A second exemption applies to assessed value between $50,000 and $75,000, and it does not apply to school taxes. The course and most exam questions treat this second exemption as $25,000. Since a 2024 constitutional amendment, the second exemption is adjusted each year for inflation, so the real figure is now slightly above $25,000. On the exam, use the textbook figure.

The gap between $25,000 and $50,000 of assessed value gets no exemption. That is why a home assessed at $40,000 gets only the first exemption, and a home has to be assessed at $75,000 or more to get the full benefit of the second.

Worked example. A homestead at 1720 Satinwood Terrace. Assessed value $300,000. Scenario millage: 6 mills for schools, 12 mills for all non-school authorities combined.

School taxes: - Taxable value for schools: $300,000 − $25,000 = $275,000. - Tax: $275,000 × 0.006 = $1,650.

Non-school taxes: - Taxable value: $300,000 − $25,000 − $25,000 = $250,000. - Tax: $250,000 × 0.012 = $3,000.

Total: $1,650 + $3,000 = $4,650.

Compare that to the same house with no homestead: $300,000 × 0.018 = $5,400. The exemption saved $750.

Worked example, the gap. A small homestead condominium at 40 Sea Oats Road is assessed at $60,000. All millage combined is 18 mills, of which 6 are school.

  • First exemption: $25,000 off everything.
  • Second exemption: applies only to the value between $50,000 and $75,000. This home has $10,000 of value in that band ($60,000 − $50,000). So the second exemption is $10,000, not $25,000, and only on non-school taxes.
  • School taxable: $60,000 − $25,000 = $35,000 × 0.006 = $210.
  • Non-school taxable: $60,000 − $25,000 − $10,000 = $25,000 × 0.012 = $300.
  • Total: $510.

Florida also has smaller exemptions for widows and widowers, the blind, persons with disabilities, and certain senior citizens with limited income (by local option), and larger or total exemptions for some disabled veterans. Take the amounts from the textbook.

The trap: applying the second $25,000 to school taxes. It never applies to school taxes. The other trap: dates. Residency on January 1; application by March 1.

Save Our Homes

Once a property has a homestead exemption, Florida's Save Our Homes amendment limits how much its assessed value can rise each year: 3 percent or the change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is less. The cap starts the year after the homestead is first granted; in the first year, assessed value is set at just value.

The cap limits assessed value, not just value. Just value keeps tracking the market. Over time the gap between the two grows, and that gap is the owner's Save Our Homes benefit.

Worked example. A homestead at 2405 Sea Oats Road.

Year 1 (first year of homestead): just value $300,000. Assessed value = $300,000.

Year 2: the market rises 12 percent. Just value = $336,000. CPI rose 2.9 percent. - Lesser of 3 percent and 2.9 percent = 2.9 percent. - Assessed value: $300,000 × 1.029 = $308,700.

Year 3: the market rises another 8 percent. Just value = $362,880. CPI rose 4.1 percent. - Lesser of 3 percent and 4.1 percent = 3 percent. - Assessed value: $308,700 × 1.03 = $317,961.

Save Our Homes benefit in year 3: $362,880 − $317,961 = $44,919.

Note also: if the market falls, assessed value can still go up by the capped amount as long as it stays below just value. The cap limits increases; it does not promise decreases.

When the home is sold, the cap does not transfer to the buyer. On January 1 after the sale, the property is reassessed at just value.

Non-homestead property has a separate cap: its assessed value cannot rise more than 10 percent a year, and that cap does not apply to school taxes.

The trap: the buyer who looks at the seller's tax bill. A buyer of the Sea Oats Road home who budgets based on the seller's bill will be surprised, because the next January 1 the assessed value resets to market value. A licensee who tells a buyer "the taxes are $4,600" without explaining the reset is inviting a misrepresentation complaint.

Portability

Florida lets a homestead owner carry the Save Our Homes benefit to a new Florida homestead. The transferable benefit is capped at $500,000. The owner must establish the new homestead within the window Florida law allows — currently up to three years after leaving the old homestead — and apply for portability along with the new homestead exemption. Take the exact counting rule for that window from the textbook.

How much transfers depends on whether the owner is upsizing or downsizing.

Upsizing (new home's just value is equal or higher): the full benefit transfers, up to the cap, and is subtracted from the new home's just value.

Downsizing (new home's just value is lower): a percentage transfers — the same percentage the benefit was of the old home's just value.

Worked example, upsizing. An owner leaves a homestead with just value $500,000 and assessed value $320,000. - Benefit: $500,000 − $320,000 = $180,000. - New homestead at 77 Satinwood Terrace, just value $650,000. - New assessed value: $650,000 − $180,000 = $470,000.

Worked example, downsizing. Same owner, same $180,000 benefit, but buys a condominium with just value $250,000. - Benefit as a percentage of the old just value: $180,000 ÷ $500,000 = 36 percent. - Transferable benefit: $250,000 × 0.36 = $90,000. - New assessed value: $250,000 − $90,000 = $160,000.

Then the homestead exemption is subtracted from the new assessed value as usual, and Save Our Homes starts capping increases again.

The trap: portability transferring to someone else. It belongs to the owner and moves with the owner to the owner's next Florida homestead. The buyer of the old house gets nothing.

The TRIM notice and appeals

In August, the property appraiser mails each owner a Notice of Proposed Property Taxes, known as the TRIM notice — "Truth in Millage." It shows the just value, assessed value, exemptions and taxable value, the proposed millage from each taxing authority, what the tax would be if each authority kept its rate unchanged, and the dates of the public budget hearings.

It is not a bill. It is the owner's chance to object before the rates are final.

An owner who disagrees with the value can first talk to the property appraiser informally, then file a petition with the value adjustment board. The petition is due within 25 days after the TRIM notice is mailed. An owner can also go to circuit court.

The trap: "the TRIM notice is the tax bill." It arrives in August. The bill comes in the fall, from the tax collector.

Paying the bill: the discount schedule

The tax collector mails bills around November 1. Florida rewards early payment:

  • Paid in November: 4 percent discount.
  • Paid in December: 3 percent.
  • Paid in January: 2 percent.
  • Paid in February: 1 percent.
  • Paid in March: no discount, full amount.
  • Unpaid on April 1: delinquent.

Worked example. The 1720 Satinwood Terrace bill is $4,650.

  • Paid November 20: $4,650 × 0.96 = $4,464. Saves $186.
  • Paid December 15: $4,650 × 0.97 = $4,510.50.
  • Paid January 10: $4,650 × 0.98 = $4,557.
  • Paid February 5: $4,650 × 0.99 = $4,603.50.
  • Paid March 31: $4,650.

Owners can also enroll in an installment plan that spreads the payment over quarterly payments with a discount built in; take the enrollment rules from the textbook.

The trap: delinquency on March 31. March is full price, not late. Delinquency starts April 1.

Tax certificates and tax deeds

When taxes go unpaid, the county does not lose the money and the owner does not immediately lose the house. Instead:

The tax certificate. The tax collector advertises the delinquent parcels and, usually by June 1, holds a sale of tax certificates. A certificate is not ownership; it is a lien. An investor who buys one pays the owner's delinquent tax to the county, and in return earns interest when the owner eventually pays. Bidders compete by bidding the interest rate down from a maximum of 18 percent. The lowest rate wins.

The owner can redeem at any time before a tax deed is issued by paying the tax, the interest and the costs.

The tax deed. If the owner does not redeem, the certificate holder can apply for a tax deed after two years from April 1 of the year the certificate was issued. The property then goes to a public auction. The certificate holder is paid from the proceeds. A certificate on which no tax deed application has been made expires seven years after it was issued.

Worked example. An owner at 1155 Sea Oats Road does not pay a $3,000 tax bill. The certificate sells at a winning bid of 6 percent. One year later the owner redeems. - Interest: $3,000 × 0.06 = $180, plus costs. - The investor recovers $3,000 plus $180 plus costs.

Florida law sets a minimum charge on a redeemed certificate in some cases, regardless of the bid rate; take the details from the textbook.

The trap: "the certificate buyer owns the property." The buyer owns a lien that earns interest. Ownership changes only through a tax deed sale.

Special assessments

A special assessment is a charge against specific parcels to pay for a public improvement that benefits them — a new sidewalk, a sewer line, a seawall, street lighting. Unlike ad valorem tax, it is based on benefit, not value. It is often computed by the front foot or per parcel, and owners commonly pay it in installments over several years. It is a lien on the property until paid. Homestead exemptions do not reduce it.

Worked example. A city installs a sewer line along Satinwood Terrace for a total assessable cost of $480,000. The street has 3,200 front feet of property.

  • Cost per front foot: $480,000 ÷ 3,200 = $150.
  • A lot with 80 feet of frontage: 80 × $150 = $12,000.
  • Payable over 10 years in equal principal installments: $12,000 ÷ 10 = $1,200 a year, plus whatever interest the city charges.

If the frontage is shared — a corner lot or an assessment split between two sides of the street — the question will say so. Read which side pays.

The trap: applying the homestead exemption to a special assessment. Exemptions reduce ad valorem tax only. A homestead owner pays the full special assessment.

Federal income tax: the homeowner

Florida has no state personal income tax, so this part is all federal.

A homeowner may generally deduct mortgage interest and property taxes if itemizing, subject to federal limits that change with tax law; take the current limits from the textbook. Rent paid on a primary residence is not deductible. Repairs to a personal residence are not deductible. Depreciation on a personal residence is not allowed.

When a home is sold, gain is calculated this way:

  1. Basis = purchase price + certain buying costs (title insurance, recording fees, some closing costs).
  2. Adjusted basis = basis + capital improvements (a new roof, an addition, a pool) − any depreciation taken. Repairs do not add to basis.
  3. Amount realized = sales price − selling costs (commission, seller's closing costs).
  4. Gain = amount realized − adjusted basis.

Worked example. 2405 Sea Oats Road. - Bought for $280,000 with $6,000 of qualifying closing costs. Basis: $286,000. - Added a new roof for $18,000. Adjusted basis: $286,000 + $18,000 = $304,000. - Painted the interior for $5,000. That is a repair. No change. - Sold for $520,000. Commission and seller costs: $31,000. Amount realized: $489,000. - Gain: $489,000 − $304,000 = $185,000.

The home-sale exclusion. Under Section 121 of the Internal Revenue Code, a seller who owned the home and used it as a principal residence for at least two of the five years before the sale may exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or up to $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. The exclusion can generally be used once every two years.

Here, the $185,000 gain falls under the $250,000 single exclusion. No federal tax on the sale.

The trap: a loss on a personal residence is not deductible. If the Sea Oats Road house had sold at a loss, the owner would get no tax deduction. Losses are deductible only on investment or business property.

Federal income tax: the investor

An investment property follows the same gain calculation, with three differences.

Depreciation. The owner deducts part of the building's cost each year, even though it may be gaining value. Land is never depreciated. Federal law sets the recovery period at 27.5 years for residential rental property and 39 years for nonresidential (commercial) property, using straight-line depreciation.

Worked example. A rental duplex at 3020 Satinwood Terrace bought for $450,000, of which $90,000 is land. - Depreciable basis: $450,000 − $90,000 = $360,000. - Annual depreciation: $360,000 ÷ 27.5 = $13,090.91. - After five years: about $65,455 deducted. Adjusted basis: $450,000 − $65,455 = $384,545. - Sold for $520,000 net of selling costs. Gain: $520,000 − $384,545 = $135,455.

Depreciation lowered taxes every year, but it also lowered basis, which raised the gain on sale. Part of that gain is taxed as depreciation recapture; take the rate rules from the textbook.

Holding period. Property held more than one year produces long-term capital gain, taxed at lower federal rates than ordinary income. Held one year or less, the gain is short-term and taxed as ordinary income. Take the rate figures from the textbook.

No home-sale exclusion. Section 121 is for a principal residence only.

The like-kind exchange (Section 1031). An investor who sells investment or business real estate and buys other investment or business real estate can defer the gain. The rules the exam uses:

  • Both properties must be held for investment or business use. A personal residence does not qualify.
  • The replacement property must be identified within 45 days of the sale and acquired within 180 days.
  • Cash or debt relief the investor receives, called boot, is taxable.
  • The basis of the old property carries over into the new property. The gain is postponed, not forgiven.

Worked example. The duplex owner sells for $520,000 with an adjusted basis of $384,545, and buys a $700,000 fourplex through a qualified exchange, taking no cash out. The $135,455 gain is deferred. The fourplex's basis is the old adjusted basis plus the additional money invested: $384,545 + ($700,000 − $520,000) = $564,545, not $700,000.

The trap: "a 1031 exchange avoids tax." It defers tax. The deferred gain is built into the lower basis of the replacement property and comes due when that property is sold in a taxable sale.

Installment sales

When a seller finances the sale and receives payments over more than one year, the gain can generally be reported in proportion to the payments received each year, rather than all in the year of sale. The idea: the seller pays tax as the money actually comes in.

Worked example. A seller with a $100,000 gain on a $400,000 sale (25 percent of the price is gain) receives $80,000 in the year of sale. Gain reported that year: $80,000 × 0.25 = $20,000. The rest is reported as later payments arrive.

The trap: installment reporting reduces the total tax. It spreads out when the gain is reported. The total gain is the same.

How to use this chapter

Read the property tax half first and work the 1720 Satinwood Terrace homestead example by hand, splitting school and non-school taxes, before opening the chapter. The federal half reads quickly once basis and gain are clear. If a number in the textbook differs from anything here — especially the homestead amounts, which have changed recently and may change again with the November 2026 ballot — the textbook wins.
