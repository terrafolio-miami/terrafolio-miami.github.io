Chapter 15 — The Real Estate Market and Analysis

What this chapter is. The economics of real estate: what makes it different from other markets, what moves prices, and how a licensee reads a market and, at the end, prices a single property within it. There is almost no law here and very little math. The exam cares because the chapter has fixed vocabulary lists, three economic characteristics and three physical ones, and the questions are mostly "which characteristic is this?" The chapter also sets up Chapter 16 (appraisal), which uses the same supply-and-demand thinking.

What a market is, and why real estate's is different

A market is wherever buyers and sellers come together to exchange. In an efficient market, like a stock exchange, the product is uniform, information is public and instant, and prices adjust in seconds.

The real estate market is the opposite on almost every count:

  • Local. A house cannot move to where the buyers are, so prices in Doral say little about prices in Ocala.
  • Stratified. It splits into submarkets by price range, property type and neighborhood. Luxury condos and starter homes in the same zip code are different markets.
  • Slow to adjust. Building new supply takes years (land, permits, construction), so a jump in demand shows up first as higher prices, and only later as more houses.
  • Imperfect information. Every property is different, many sales are private, and buyers and sellers are rarely experts. That gap is the reason licensees exist.
  • Large transactions, usually financed. So the cost and availability of credit move the market more than in most others.

The scene: a new employer announces 2,000 jobs near Wekiva Bend. Within months, rents and asking prices climb. New subdivisions do not open for two or three years. By then, if the jobs did not all arrive, the new supply can overshoot.

The trap: calling the real estate market "efficient" or "national." It is local and inefficient. Those two words are in the answer key almost every time.

Supply and demand

The basic law: when demand rises and supply stays put, prices rise. When supply rises and demand stays put, prices fall. Equilibrium is the price where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer.

In real estate, supply is inelastic in the short run: it cannot respond quickly to price. That is why demand shocks show up as price swings.

Two market states the exam names:

  • Seller's market: more buyers than properties. Prices rise, homes sell fast, buyers compete, sellers dictate terms.
  • Buyer's market: more properties than buyers. Prices soften, homes sit, buyers negotiate concessions.

A common way to tell which is which is months of inventory: the number of homes for sale divided by the number selling per month. Fewer months means a tighter, seller-leaning market; more months means a buyer-leaning one. The book may give a figure for "balanced"; use its figure.

Worked scene: in the Sabal Palm Avenue neighborhood, 180 homes are listed and 30 sell each month. 180 ÷ 30 = 6 months of inventory. If listings drop to 90 at the same sales pace, it is 3 months, and sellers have the advantage.

Months of inventory is a ratio, not a verdict. It describes one submarket at one moment, so the figure for condos on the same street can point the opposite way from the figure for houses.

The trap: price is the result, not the cause. A question that says "prices rose, so demand rose" has it backwards. Demand rose (or supply fell), so prices rose.

Factors that move supply

What determines how much real estate is offered for sale or built:

  • Availability and cost of land, especially land with utilities and the right zoning.
  • Construction costs: materials and labor.
  • Availability and cost of financing for builders.
  • Government regulation: zoning, building codes, impact fees, permitting timelines, environmental restrictions.
  • Labor supply in the trades.

Florida-specific supply factors the chapter lens fits: property insurance and flood insurance costs, which affect what builders can sell and owners can carry; wind-resistant building codes; and Florida's post-2021 condominium safety laws, passed after the Surfside collapse, that require structural inspections and reserve funding for older buildings. Those laws push special assessments onto condo owners and change how much older condo inventory comes to market. Take the specific requirements and deadlines from the textbook or from current law, since they have been amended more than once.

The scene: a 1970s condo tower on Manatee Avenue faces a large special assessment after its structural inspection. Several owners list at once to avoid paying it. Supply in that building jumps and prices there fall, even while single-family prices a mile away hold steady. A stratified market, moving in two directions at once.

The trap: regulation counts as a supply factor even though no one "makes" or "sells" anything because of it. Stricter codes and slower permits reduce supply.

Factors that move demand

What determines how many people want to buy or rent:

  • Population: growth, migration, household formation. Florida's population inflow from other states is the textbook example of steady demand pressure.
  • Employment and income: new jobs and rising wages increase demand; layoffs reduce it.
  • Interest rates and credit availability: lower rates and easier qualifying raise what buyers can afford, which raises demand. Rising rates do the opposite even when nothing else changes.
  • Consumer confidence and expectations: buyers who expect prices to rise buy sooner.
  • Taxes: property tax levels and exemptions affect what owning costs.
  • Demographics and tastes: retirees, remote workers, household size.

Florida-specific: the Save Our Homes cap limits annual increases in the assessed value of a homestead to 3 percent or the change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is lower. Long-time owners can end up paying taxes on an assessed value well below market value. Portability lets an owner who sells and buys a new Florida homestead carry up to $500,000 of that accumulated benefit to the new home. Both reduce the "lock-in" effect that would otherwise keep long-time owners from moving, so they shape how much existing inventory comes to market. The details belong to Chapter 18.

The scene: a couple who bought on Loxahatchee Drive twenty years ago want a smaller home nearby. Without portability, moving would reset their taxes to full market value. With portability they carry much of the benefit with them, so they list. One more house on the market because of a tax rule.

The trap: interest rates. The exam treats them as a demand factor for buyers (affordability) and a supply factor for builders (cost of construction loans). Read which side the question is about.

Economic characteristics of real estate

The textbook's first list. These describe how real estate behaves as an investment and a commodity:

  • Scarcity. Land is limited in supply, especially land in a given location. Total land is fixed; usable land in demand areas is scarce.
  • Improvements (modification). Any improvement to one parcel, or near it, changes the value of that parcel and of surrounding ones. A new highway interchange, a school, or a landfill all change values on neighboring land.
  • Permanence of investment (fixity). Money spent on improvements, such as buildings, roads and sewers, is fixed in place for a long time and cannot be recovered quickly. Buildings, once built, stay, which is why supply responds so slowly.
  • Area preference (situs). People's preferences for a particular location. It is the single largest driver of value: identical houses in two neighborhoods sell at different prices because of where they are.

The scene: two identical three-bedroom houses, one on Flamingo Road a block from the water and one inland near the interstate, built by the same builder in the same year, sell $140,000 apart. Nothing about the houses explains it. Area preference does.

The trap: situs is economic, not physical. It is not about the location itself (that would be immobility); it is about people's preference for the location. The exam tests this distinction more than any other in the chapter.

Physical characteristics of real estate

The textbook's second list. These describe the land itself:

  • Immobility. Land cannot be moved. That is why real estate markets are local, and why real estate is governed by the laws of the state where it sits.
  • Indestructibility. Land cannot be destroyed. Buildings can be; the land beneath them remains. It is why land does not depreciate for tax purposes.
  • Nonhomogeneity (uniqueness). No two parcels are identical; at a minimum, each occupies a different location. That is why a buyer can sue for specific performance of a real estate contract: money is not a substitute for a unique parcel.

The scene: a hurricane takes the house on a lot on Citrus Ridge Parkway down to the slab. The building is gone. The lot, its location and its legal description are unchanged. Indestructibility is about the land, not the house.

The trap: the two lists get scrambled. Scarcity, improvements, permanence, area preference are economic. Immobility, indestructibility, uniqueness are physical. One memory aid: the physical three describe what the land is; the economic four describe how people and money treat it.

Business cycles and real estate cycles

The general economy moves in cycles with four phases:

  1. Expansion (recovery and growth): employment, income and spending rise.
  2. Peak: growth tops out.
  3. Contraction (recession): activity declines.
  4. Trough: the bottom, before the next expansion.

Real estate follows its own cycle, tied to the general one but not in lockstep. Because building takes years, construction tends to keep going after demand has peaked, which produces overbuilding, then a glut, then falling prices and a halt in construction, then a slow absorption of the excess. Real estate cycles tend to be longer than general business cycles and to lag them.

The chapter also distinguishes seasonal variation (patterns within the year, such as Florida's winter demand from seasonal residents), long-term trends (decades of population growth), and random changes from one-time events (a hurricane, a plant closing).

The scene: a condo developer on Wekiva Bend breaks ground at the height of a boom. Three years later the towers open into a slowdown, and the building sits half-empty while prices fall. The lag between decision and delivery is the real estate cycle.

The trap: "real estate cycles lead the general economy." In the textbook framing they tend to lag it. Seasonal variation is also not a cycle; it repeats within a year.

Market indicators

What a licensee or analyst watches to read where a market is heading:

  • Building permits and housing starts: the best early sign of future supply.
  • Sales volume and price trends: how many properties are selling and at what price.
  • Days on market: how long listings take to sell.
  • Inventory and months of supply.
  • Vacancy rates (for rentals): rising vacancy means supply is outrunning demand.
  • Absorption rate: how quickly new space or new units are leased or sold.
  • Interest rates and mortgage applications.
  • Employment and population data.
  • Foreclosure and distressed-sale levels.

Worked scene: a new apartment complex on Manatee Avenue has 240 units and leases 20 a month. 240 ÷ 20 = 12 months to absorb. If a competing complex with 300 units opens next door at the same time, both slow down.

The trap: building permits are a leading indicator, a sign of supply to come. Sales already closed are lagging. The exam uses "which indicator would best predict" as the cue for permits.

Market analysis by property type

The chapter applies the same supply-and-demand analysis to each market segment, with different variables:

  • Residential: population, household formation, incomes, interest rates, school quality, commuting.
  • Commercial (retail and office): trade area population and income, traffic counts, visibility, competing space, office employment.
  • Industrial: access to highways, rail, ports and airports; labor force; utilities; zoning. Florida's ports and distribution corridors are the textbook example.
  • Agricultural: soil, water, climate, commodity prices.

A market study describes supply and demand for a property type in an area. A feasibility study goes further and asks whether a specific project will succeed financially at a specific site. The first describes the market; the second tests a plan against it.

The scene: a developer considering a self-storage facility on Loxahatchee Drive commissions a market study first (how much storage exists, how much is rented, how many households are within the trade area) and then a feasibility study (whether this site, at this land cost, with this construction budget, earns an acceptable return).

The trap: market study and feasibility study are not interchangeable. Feasibility is project-specific and financial.

From market analysis to pricing one property

The chapter ends where Chapter 16 begins: using the market to estimate the value of one property.

A comparative market analysis (CMA) is the licensee's tool. It compares the subject property with recently sold similar properties (comparables), adjusts for differences, and looks at active and expired listings to show what the market will and will not pay. It is used to help a seller set a list price or a buyer decide on an offer.

A CMA is not an appraisal. Florida law allows a licensee to prepare a CMA, and to give a broker price opinion (BPO), an estimate of probable selling price for a lender, owner or other party, including for a fee, as long as it is not referred to as an appraisal and the conditions Florida law sets are met. A licensee who wants to perform appraisals in federally related transactions needs a separate appraisal credential. Take the exact conditions for BPOs and the appraisal rules from the textbook and Chapter 16.

The scene: the owner of a house on Sabal Palm Avenue asks a sales associate what to list for. The associate pulls four sales from the last few months within a half mile, adjusts for one comparable's pool and another's smaller lot, and shows two active listings sitting unsold at higher prices. The result is a list-price range. The associate calls it a CMA, never an appraisal.

The trap: a licensee calling a CMA an "appraisal" in writing. The analysis can be good; the label is the violation.

How to use this chapter

Read the two characteristic lists here first and write them from memory, economic and physical, before opening the chapter; most of the chapter's exam questions are sorting questions on those seven words. The rest reads quickly once the supply-and-demand logic is clear.
