Chapter 14 — Real Estate Related Computations and Closing of Transactions

What this chapter is. The arithmetic of a deal and the paperwork that settles it. The exam cares because a large share of its math questions come from here, and because the closing statement is where every earlier chapter's concepts turn into dollars: the commission from the listing, the loan from Chapters 12 and 13, the Florida taxes on the deed and the mortgage. The math is not hard. What costs points is a wrong convention: the wrong day count, the wrong base for a tax, a credit entered on the wrong side. Every worked example below shows each step so the convention is visible.

One deal runs through the whole chapter.

The scene. 2240 Seminole Trail, a single-family house in Orange County. Price $385,000. The buyer, Renata Alvarez, puts down a $20,000 deposit with the contract, and gets a new conventional loan for $308,000 at 6.5 percent for 30 years. The seller, Graham Whitfield, owes $190,000 on his existing mortgage. Closing is June 15, 2026. Listing commission is 6 percent. Annual property taxes are $4,745, unpaid at closing.

The one formula

Almost every problem in the chapter is the same formula wearing different clothes:

Part = Total × Rate

Commission = price × commission rate. Down payment = price × down payment percentage. Interest = principal × interest rate. Tax = taxable amount × tax rate.

When the problem gives the part and asks for something else, rearrange:

  • Total = Part ÷ Rate
  • Rate = Part ÷ Total

Worked: a sales associate on Gumbo Limbo Lane received $5,400, which was 60 percent of what her broker received. What did the broker receive? Total = $5,400 ÷ 0.60 = $9,000.

The trap: converting the percentage. 6 percent is 0.06, not 0.6. Half a percent is 0.005. Most wrong answers on the exam are one decimal place off.

Area and measurement

The few conversions the exam uses:

  • 1 square yard = 9 square feet
  • 1 acre = 43,560 square feet
  • 1 mile = 5,280 feet
  • 1 section = 1 square mile = 640 acres

Rectangle area = length × width. Triangle area = ½ × base × height.

Worked: a lot on Ibis Court is 150 feet by 290.4 feet.

  • Step 1. Area = 150 × 290.4 = 43,560 square feet.
  • Step 2. Acres = 43,560 ÷ 43,560 = 1 acre.
  • Step 3. If the lot is priced at $2.50 a square foot: 43,560 × $2.50 = $108,900.

Front footage: a lot's price is sometimes quoted per front foot, meaning per foot of frontage along the street. The first number in a lot dimension is customarily the frontage. A 150 × 290.4 lot at $700 per front foot costs 150 × $700 = $105,000.

The trap: square feet to square yards. Divide by 9, not by 3.

Commission and commission splits

Commission is figured on the sale price, then divided in the order the money actually flows: the seller pays the listing broker, the listing broker splits with the cooperating broker, each broker pays their own associate.

Worked, at 2240 Seminole Trail:

  • Step 1. Total commission = $385,000 × 0.06 = $23,100.
  • Step 2. The listing brokerage and the buyer's brokerage split 50/50: $23,100 × 0.50 = $11,550 each.
  • Step 3. The buyer's associate, Kenji Morales, is on a 60/40 split with his broker: $11,550 × 0.60 = $6,930 to Kenji, $11,550 × 0.40 = $4,620 to his broker.

Working backward: if Kenji's $6,930 is the only figure given, divide back up the chain. $6,930 ÷ 0.60 = $11,550 (his brokerage's share). $11,550 ÷ 0.50 = $23,100 (the total). $23,100 ÷ 0.06 = $385,000 (the price).

Net listing problems: a seller who wants to net $200,000 after a 6 percent commission. The net is 94 percent of the price, so the price = $200,000 ÷ 0.94 = $212,765.96. Adding 6 percent to $200,000 ($212,000) is wrong, because the commission is figured on the price, not on the net.

Florida license-law point: commission is paid only by the employing broker to the associate. The money in Step 3 goes from the cooperating broker to Kenji's broker, never directly to Kenji.

The trap: the net-price problem. Divide by (1 − rate). Do not multiply by (1 + rate).

Loan-to-value and down payment

Loan-to-value (LTV) = loan ÷ value. The lender's "value" is the lower of the sale price or the appraised value.

Worked, at 2240 Seminole Trail, if the appraisal comes in at the price:

  • LTV = $308,000 ÷ $385,000 = 0.80 = 80 percent.
  • Down payment = $385,000 − $308,000 = $77,000.

Now suppose the appraisal comes in low, at $375,000, and the lender still lends 80 percent:

  • Step 1. The lender uses the lower figure, $375,000.
  • Step 2. Loan = $375,000 × 0.80 = $300,000.
  • Step 3. The buyer still owes the full $385,000 price. Down payment = $385,000 − $300,000 = $85,000.

The appraisal gap of $10,000 comes out of the buyer's pocket, unless the price is renegotiated.

The trap: applying the LTV to the price when the appraisal is lower. The lender never lends a percentage of a number it does not believe.

Interest and amortization

Annual interest = principal × annual rate. Monthly interest = annual interest ÷ 12.

Worked, Renata's loan:

  • Annual interest on $308,000 at 6.5 percent = $308,000 × 0.065 = $20,020.
  • Month 1 interest = $20,020 ÷ 12 = $1,668.33.

The monthly principal-and-interest payment on that loan, from an amortization table or calculator, is $1,946.77. The exam gives the payment; it does not expect the payment to be calculated from scratch.

How the first payment splits:

  • Step 1. Interest portion = $1,668.33 (above).
  • Step 2. Principal portion = $1,946.77 − $1,668.33 = $278.44.
  • Step 3. New balance = $308,000 − $278.44 = $307,721.56.
  • Month 2 repeats the steps on the new balance. Interest is slightly lower, principal slightly higher.

Points: one point = 1 percent of the loan. Two discount points on Renata's loan = $308,000 × 0.02 = $6,160.

Mortgage interest is paid in arrears. The July 1 payment covers June's interest. That is why a lender collects prepaid interest at closing, from the closing date to the end of the month. For Renata, closing June 15 through June 30 is 16 days (the buyer owns the closing day):

  • Daily interest = $20,020 ÷ 365 = $54.849 (carry three decimals; round only the final answer).
  • 16 × $54.849 = $877.59. Her first regular payment is then August 1, covering July's interest.

The trap: confusing the payment with the interest. The interest is only part of the payment, and the part shrinks every month.

Florida documentary stamp tax on deeds

Florida taxes the document that transfers real property. The tax on the deed is $0.70 per $100 of consideration (the price), or fraction of $100. In Miami-Dade County the rate is $0.60 per $100, plus a surtax of $0.45 per $100 on deeds for property other than a single-family residence, for a total of $1.05.

Customarily, the seller pays the deed stamps.

The method:

  • Step 1. Divide the price by 100. If there is any remainder, round up to the next whole number (the "or fraction thereof").
  • Step 2. Multiply by the rate.

Worked, at 2240 Seminole Trail (Orange County):

  • Step 1. $385,000 ÷ 100 = 3,850.
  • Step 2. 3,850 × $0.70 = $2,695. Seller debit.

Worked, with an uneven price of $385,050:

  • Step 1. $385,050 ÷ 100 = 3,850.5, rounded up to 3,851.
  • Step 2. 3,851 × $0.70 = $2,695.70.

Same house, if it were in Miami-Dade as a single-family residence: 3,850 × $0.60 = $2,310. A small office building in Miami-Dade at the same price: 3,850 × $1.05 = $4,042.50.

When the buyer assumes the seller's mortgage, the deed stamps are still on the full price, because the assumed debt is part of what the buyer gives for the property.

The trap: figuring deed stamps on the new loan or on the seller's equity. Deed stamps go on the price.

Florida documentary stamp tax on notes

The promissory note is taxed separately, at $0.35 per $100 of the note amount, or fraction of $100. Same rate in every county, Miami-Dade included. Customarily, the buyer (borrower) pays.

Worked, Renata's $308,000 note:

  • Step 1. $308,000 ÷ 100 = 3,080.
  • Step 2. 3,080 × $0.35 = $1,078. Buyer debit.

The Florida course treats the note tax as applying to new notes and to mortgages the buyer assumes. Confirm the textbook's wording on assumptions.

Florida intangible tax on mortgages

The nonrecurring intangible tax is a one-time tax on a new mortgage recorded against Florida real property: 2 mills, which is $0.002 per dollar ($2 per $1,000) of the loan. No rounding to the next $100; the tax is figured on the exact loan amount. Customarily, the buyer (borrower) pays.

Worked, Renata's $308,000 mortgage:

  • $308,000 × 0.002 = $616. Buyer debit.

The intangible tax applies only to new mortgages. A mortgage the buyer assumes was taxed when it was originally recorded, so it is not taxed again.

Prorations: what they are

A proration divides an expense or income between buyer and seller according to who owned the property on which days. Property taxes, rent, HOA dues and interest on an assumed loan are the usual items.

The two questions for every proration:

  1. Was it paid in advance or in arrears? If the seller already paid for days the buyer will own, the buyer owes the seller (credit seller, debit buyer). If the bill has not been paid yet and the seller owned some of the days it covers, the seller owes the buyer (debit seller, credit buyer).
  2. How many days belong to each side?

The Florida convention: the buyer owns the day of closing. The seller is responsible through the day before closing. Unless an exam question says otherwise, use that.

Florida property taxes are a natural arrears item. They cover the calendar year, January 1 through December 31, and the bill comes in November. Paying in November earns a 4 percent discount, declining by a point each month (3 percent in December, 2 in January, 1 in February), with the full amount due by March 31 and delinquent April 1. A June closing therefore almost always means this year's taxes are unpaid, and the seller owes the buyer for the days the seller owned.

Proration: the 365-day method

Divide the annual amount by 365 (366 in a leap year) to get a daily rate, then count the actual days. Florida courses and the state exam use this method as the default; check the book's statement of the convention.

Worked, taxes at 2240 Seminole Trail, $4,745 for the year, unpaid, closing June 15, 2026:

  • Step 1. Daily rate = $4,745 ÷ 365 = $13.00.
  • Step 2. Count the seller's days, January 1 through June 14 (the buyer owns June 15):
    • January 31, February 28, March 31, April 30, May 31 = 151 days
    • June 1 through 14 = 14 days
    • Total = 165 days
  • Step 3. Seller's share = 165 × $13.00 = $2,145.
  • Step 4. Entry: the taxes are unpaid, so the seller owes the buyer. Debit seller $2,145, credit buyer $2,145. The buyer will pay the full bill in November with the seller's money.

Proration: the 30-day-month method

Treat every month as 30 days and the year as 360 days (the "banker's year" or statutory year). Used when a question says so.

Worked, same taxes:

  • Step 1. Monthly rate = $4,745 ÷ 12 = $395.4167. Daily rate = $4,745 ÷ 360 = $13.1806.
  • Step 2. Seller's time: 5 full months (January through May) plus 14 days.
  • Step 3. 5 × $395.4167 = $1,977.08. 14 × $13.1806 = $184.53.
  • Step 4. Seller's share = $1,977.08 + $184.53 = $2,161.61.

Same deal, $16.61 more than the 365-day answer. The exam's wrong choices are usually the other method's answer, so the method has to be fixed before the arithmetic starts.

Proration: a prepaid item

Rent is collected in advance, so it runs the other way.

Worked: Graham also sells a rented condo on Live Oak Boulevard, closing June 15. He collected June's rent, $2,400, on June 1.

  • Step 1. June has 30 days. Daily rent = $2,400 ÷ 30 = $80.
  • Step 2. The buyer owns June 15 through June 30 = 16 days.
  • Step 3. Buyer's share = 16 × $80 = $1,280.
  • Step 4. The seller holds rent that belongs to the buyer. Debit seller $1,280, credit buyer $1,280.

The tenant's security deposit is not prorated at all. The whole deposit transfers to the buyer, who will owe it back to the tenant: debit seller, credit buyer, in full.

The trap: counting the closing day. "June 15 through June 30" is 16 days, not 15. When in doubt, count on fingers.

The closing statement

The closing statement lists every dollar for each side as a debit (what that party owes) or a credit (what that party receives or has already paid). The rules:

Double entries appear on both sides, once as a debit to one party and once as a credit to the other. The purchase price and all prorations are double entries.

Single entries appear on one side only, because the money comes from or goes to someone outside the buyer-seller pair:

  • Buyer's deposit: credit buyer only. It is already in escrow and counts toward what the buyer owes.
  • Buyer's new loan: credit buyer only. The lender is supplying it.
  • Seller's mortgage payoff: debit seller only. It goes to the seller's lender.
  • Each party's own expenses (commission, doc stamps, title, recording): debit that party only.

Who customarily pays what in Florida (the contract controls; these are the defaults the course teaches):

  • Seller: deed doc stamps, the commission, payoff of the seller's liens.
  • Buyer: note doc stamps, intangible tax, lender fees, recording the mortgage.
  • Owner's title insurance policy: in most of Florida, the seller selects and pays. In Miami-Dade and Broward, and a few other counties, custom runs the other way and the buyer does. Take the county list from the textbook.

The closing process in Florida

Florida residential closings are usually handled by a title company or a real estate attorney acting as the closing agent. There is no requirement that an attorney conduct the closing. The closing agent orders the title search, clears liens, prepares the closing statement, collects the funds, pays the taxes, and records the deed and mortgage in the county public records.

For a financed sale, the federal TRID rules apply: the borrower must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before consummation of the loan. That is the lender's form, and the figures on it should match the closing agent's statement.

The licensee's role: monitor deadlines, make sure inspections, appraisal and financing contingencies are met or waived in writing, and review the statement for errors before closing. A licensee does not give tax or legal advice about the statement.

The scene: three days before a closing on Live Oak Boulevard, the buyer's associate compares the Closing Disclosure with the title agent's settlement figures and finds the tax proration entered as a credit to the seller. Caught then, the correction is a phone call. Caught at the table, it delays the closing.

The trap: assuming an attorney must close. In Florida, a title agent closing is ordinary practice.

How to use this chapter

Read the worked examples with a pencil and redo each one on paper before opening the matching section of the chapter; the arithmetic sticks only when done by hand. When the textbook's convention differs from anything here (day count, rounding, who pays), the textbook's version is the one the exam uses.
