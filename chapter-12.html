Chapter 12 — Residential Mortgages

What this chapter is. The legal machinery behind a home loan: what the borrower signs, what the lender gets, what happens when payments stop, and which federal laws police the lender along the way. The exam cares because nearly every sale a licensee touches is financed, and because the vocabulary here is precise. Mortgagor and mortgagee, assume and subject to, acceleration and alienation: each pair is a coin flip for anyone who learned it loosely, and the exam is built to catch the loose version.

Mortgage theory: lien, title, intermediate

There are three ways a state can describe what a lender holds while a loan is outstanding.

In a title-theory state, the borrower technically hands legal title to the lender (or a trustee) until the loan is paid off, and keeps only the right to use the property. In a lien-theory state, the borrower keeps legal title the whole time and the lender holds only a lien, a claim against the property that can be enforced through a court if the borrower defaults. An intermediate-theory state starts as lien theory and shifts title to the lender on default.

Florida is a lien-theory state. The buyer of a house on Banyan Street who finances 90 percent of the price owns that house the day of closing. The bank has a recorded lien, not the deed.

Why Florida does it this way: the lender is protected by the right to foreclose, which runs through a judge, so there is no need for the lender to hold title. Lien theory also fits Florida's strong protection of homeowners, which runs through the whole chapter.

The trap: a question that says the lender "owns the property until the loan is paid." That is title theory. In Florida, the borrower owns it; the lender has a lien.

The promissory note

Two documents come out of every mortgage loan, and the exam wants them kept separate.

The promissory note is the borrower's personal promise to repay: the amount, the interest rate, the payment schedule, and what happens on late payment. It is the evidence of the debt. The borrower who signs it is the maker; the lender is the payee. A note is a negotiable instrument, which means the lender can sell it to someone else, and the buyer of the note (the holder) can enforce it.

The note can exist without a mortgage. A loan from a relative on a signed note, with nothing pledged, is an unsecured debt. What turns it into a mortgage loan is the second document.

Florida-specific: a promissory note secured by Florida real estate carries documentary stamp tax on notes. The rate and the arithmetic are in Chapter 14. The point here is that the tax attaches to the note, not to the deed.

The trap: the note and the mortgage are not the same paper. The note is the promise to pay. The mortgage is the pledge of the house as security for that promise. If the exam asks which document creates the personal obligation to repay, the answer is the note.

The mortgage

The mortgage is the security instrument. It pledges the property as collateral for the debt in the note, and it gives the lender the right to foreclose if the borrower breaks the note's promise.

Who is who: the borrower gives the mortgage, so the borrower is the mortgagor. The lender receives it, so the lender is the mortgagee. The "-or" gives, the "-ee" receives, the same pattern as grantor and grantee on a deed.

A Florida mortgage has to be in writing, signed by the mortgagor, and recorded in the public records of the county where the property sits, so the lender's lien has priority against later claims. Recording also triggers Florida's nonrecurring intangible tax on the mortgage (again, rate and arithmetic in Chapter 14).

Florida homestead matters here. When a married owner mortgages the home the family lives in, Florida's constitution requires the spouse to join in signing the mortgage, even if the spouse is not on title. Homestead protects a Florida home from forced sale by most creditors, but it does not protect against a mortgage the owner voluntarily signed on the home, against unpaid property taxes, or against debts for the home's purchase, improvement or repair.

The scene: Daniela owns a house on Ridgewood Avenue in her own name, bought before she married. She lives there with her husband, Tomas. She applies for a home equity loan against it alone. The title company will insist on Tomas's signature on the mortgage, because the house is their homestead.

The trap: "the mortgagee borrows the money." Backwards. The borrower is the mortgagor. And "homestead protects the home from foreclosure by the lender." It does not, when the lender holds a mortgage the owners signed.

The clauses

A mortgage is a long document, and the exam tests a handful of its clauses by name.

  • Acceleration clause. On default, the lender can declare the entire remaining balance due at once, not just the missed payments. Without it, the lender could only sue for each payment as it came due. Every foreclosure starts with acceleration.
  • Defeasance clause. When the debt is paid in full, the lender's lien is defeated and the lender has to release it. This is what produces the satisfaction of mortgage.
  • Alienation clause, also called due-on-sale. If the borrower sells or transfers the property, the lender can call the whole loan due. It is what stops a buyer from quietly taking over a seller's low-rate loan without the lender's approval.
  • Prepayment clause. States whether the borrower can pay early and whether there is a penalty. A prepayment penalty charges for paying early; a lock-in clause forbids early payment altogether.
  • Escalation clause. Lets the lender raise the interest rate on stated events, such as late payment or a change in an index.
  • Subordination clause. The lender agrees that its lien will rank below a later lien. Common with land loans, where the land lender subordinates so a construction lender will agree to fund the building.
  • Release clause, or partial release. Used in a blanket mortgage covering several parcels: as each lot is sold and a stated amount is paid, that lot is released from the lien.
  • Escrow (impound) clause. The borrower pays a monthly share of property taxes and insurance into an account the lender controls, so the lender knows those bills get paid.

The scene: a developer borrows against twelve lots on Mangrove Circle under one blanket mortgage. Each time a lot sells, the partial release clause lets the developer pay down a set amount and deliver that one lot free of the lender's lien.

The trap: acceleration and alienation are both "the lender calls the whole loan due," and the exam swaps them. Acceleration is triggered by default. Alienation is triggered by transfer. A borrower who is current on every payment can still have the loan called if the alienation clause is triggered by a sale.

Buying a property that already has a mortgage

When a seller's house still carries a loan, a buyer can deal with that loan in three ways, and the liability consequences are different for each.

Pay it off. The buyer gets new financing or pays cash; the seller's loan is paid at closing. Clean.

Take subject to the existing mortgage. The buyer takes title and starts making the payments but does not sign on to the note. If the buyer stops paying, the lender can foreclose and take the house, and the buyer loses the equity, but the lender cannot go after the buyer personally. The seller stays fully liable on the note.

Assume the mortgage. The buyer signs on and becomes personally liable for the debt. The seller, however, remains secondarily liable unless the lender releases the seller. If the buyer defaults and the foreclosure sale falls short, the lender can pursue the buyer first and then the seller.

Novation. The lender agrees to substitute the buyer for the seller and releases the seller entirely. The original obligation is replaced by a new one. This is the only way the seller walks away clean while the loan continues.

Either way, the buyer and the closing agent need to know the exact balance. That comes from an estoppel certificate (also called an estoppel letter), a statement from the lender of the balance, the rate and whether the loan is in good standing. "Estoppel" because once the lender signs it, the lender is stopped from later claiming a different balance.

The scene: the Okafors sell a house on Seagrape Drive to a buyer who assumes their loan. Two years later the buyer defaults, the house sells at foreclosure for less than the balance, and the lender sues. The Okafors are on the hook for the shortfall because the lender never released them. A novation would have prevented it.

The trap: "subject to" versus "assume." The buyer who takes subject to has no personal liability. The buyer who assumes does. In both, the seller is still liable unless there is a release or novation. And in both, the alienation clause may let the lender block the transfer in the first place.

Assignment and satisfaction

A lender can sell a loan. The transfer of the note and mortgage to a new holder is an assignment, and the new holder records it so the public record shows who holds the lien. The borrower's obligation does not change; only the address on the payment coupon does.

When the loan is paid off, the lender must deliver a satisfaction of mortgage (in some places called a release), which the borrower records to clear the lien from the public record. Florida sets a deadline for the lender to deliver it after payoff; take the number of days from the textbook.

The trap: a paid-off loan whose satisfaction was never recorded still shows as an open lien. The title search for the next sale will catch it. Payment ends the debt; recording the satisfaction ends the cloud on title.

Default and foreclosure in Florida

Foreclosure is how a lender turns its lien into money. States do it one of two ways: judicial foreclosure, through a lawsuit, or nonjudicial (power of sale) foreclosure, where a trustee sells the property without going to court.

Florida is a judicial foreclosure state. Every residential foreclosure in Florida goes through the circuit court. The sequence, in order:

  1. The borrower defaults, and the lender sends the notice required by the mortgage, then accelerates the debt.
  2. The lender files a foreclosure complaint and records a lis pendens, a notice of pending lawsuit, in the county records. Anyone who buys or lends against the property after that takes it subject to the outcome of the case.
  3. The borrower is served and can answer and defend.
  4. If the lender wins, the court enters a final judgment of foreclosure setting the amount owed and a sale date.
  5. The clerk of court conducts a public sale to the highest bidder. In most Florida counties this is now an online auction.
  6. The clerk issues a certificate of sale, and after the period for objections passes, a certificate of title to the winning bidder.

Equity of redemption. Until the foreclosure sale is final, the borrower can stop it by paying the full amount owed, including costs. In Florida, the right to redeem runs until the clerk files the certificate of sale, or until a later time if the judgment sets one. Florida does not give the borrower a statutory right to buy the house back after the sale, which some other states do.

Why Florida does it this way: a court supervises the taking of someone's home. It is slower and more expensive for lenders, and it gives borrowers time and a forum to raise defenses.

The scene: a homeowner on Cypress Bend falls behind in the spring. By the time the lis pendens is recorded she has a buyer lined up. She can still sell, pay off the lender in full from the proceeds, and end the case, because the sale has not happened.

The trap: an answer choice describing a trustee's sale with no court involvement. That is nonjudicial foreclosure, used in deed-of-trust states. Not Florida.

Deficiency judgments, deed in lieu, short sale

If the foreclosure sale brings less than the debt, the gap is the deficiency. Florida allows the lender to ask the court for a deficiency judgment against the borrower personally for that amount. Florida law limits how long the lender has to pursue it after the sale; take the period from the textbook.

Two ways around a full foreclosure:

A deed in lieu of foreclosure is the borrower voluntarily deeding the property to the lender in exchange for the lender dropping the foreclosure. Faster and cheaper for both. The catch for the lender: it takes the property subject to any junior liens, which a foreclosure would have wiped out. So lenders accept a deed in lieu mainly when title is otherwise clean.

A short sale is a sale to a third-party buyer for less than the loan balance, with the lender's approval to accept the reduced payoff. Whether the lender waives the remaining deficiency is a separate negotiated point and should be in writing.

The scene: a seller on Tamarind Road owes $310,000 and the best offer is $285,000. The lender approves the short sale. Unless the approval letter waives the $25,000 shortfall, the lender can still pursue it.

The trap: assuming a short sale or a deed in lieu automatically ends personal liability. It does only if the lender agrees to that in writing.

Mortgage fraud and predatory lending

The chapter names the schemes, and the exam uses the names:

  • Straw buyer: someone lends their name and credit to buy a property for someone else who could not qualify.
  • Illegal property flipping: buying a property and reselling it quickly at a price inflated by a falsified appraisal.
  • Equity skimming: buying from a distressed owner, collecting rent, never paying the mortgage, and walking away.
  • Inflated appraisal and falsified income or employment on a loan application.
  • Predatory lending: loans designed to profit from the borrower's failure, such as excessive fees, repeated refinancing that strips equity ("loan flipping"), or steering a qualified borrower into a worse loan.

A licensee who knowingly takes part in any of this faces federal criminal charges and license discipline. A licensee who notices a red flag, such as cash passing outside closing or a contract price that differs from the one given to the lender, has to refuse to take part.

The trap: "the buyer is getting a gift from the seller, but we won't show it on the closing statement." Any money that changes hands in the deal and is hidden from the lender is mortgage fraud, whatever it is called.

Federal lending laws

Several federal laws govern residential lending. The exam tests which law does what.

RESPA (Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act). Applies to federally related loans on one-to-four-family residential property, which is nearly all of them. It requires disclosure of settlement costs, limits how much a lender can hold in an escrow account, and prohibits kickbacks and referral fees between settlement providers. A title company that pays a licensee for sending it business is a RESPA violation, as is the licensee who takes the payment.

TILA (Truth in Lending Act) and Regulation Z. Requires the lender to disclose the cost of credit, above all the annual percentage rate (APR), which folds the interest and most loan fees into one comparable rate. TILA also governs advertising: if an ad mentions certain specific credit terms, such as the down payment, the number of payments, the payment amount or the finance charge, it triggers full disclosure, including the APR. "Low monthly payments" is general and triggers nothing; "$500 down" is specific and triggers full disclosure. TILA also gives a three-business-day right of rescission on certain refinances and home equity loans secured by a primary residence. There is no rescission right on a loan to buy the home.

TRID (TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure). Combines the two laws' disclosures into two forms. The Loan Estimate goes to the borrower within three business days of application. The Closing Disclosure must be received at least three business days before consummation of the loan. Certain changes after the Closing Disclosure is delivered restart the three-day wait.

ECOA (Equal Credit Opportunity Act). Prohibits discrimination in lending based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age (if old enough to contract), or because income comes from public assistance. Note the list is not the Fair Housing Act's list: ECOA adds marital status, age and public assistance income.

Licensing of mortgage professionals. The federal SAFE Act requires mortgage loan originators to be licensed or registered and listed in a national registry. In Florida, mortgage brokers, lenders and loan originators are licensed by the Office of Financial Regulation under Chapter 494 of the Florida Statutes, not by FREC. A real estate license does not authorize a licensee to arrange a mortgage loan for a fee.

The scene: a sales associate selling a condo on Banyan Street posts an ad reading "Only $9,000 down, 30-year financing." Both are specific credit terms. The ad now has to carry the APR and the other required disclosures, or it violates Regulation Z.

The trap: the right of rescission. The exam will offer it on a purchase loan. It applies only to certain loans on a home the borrower already owns and lives in, like a refinance, never to the loan used to buy it.

How to use this chapter

Read the theory, note and mortgage sections first and open the textbook to the same pages; those set the vocabulary for everything else. The foreclosure sequence and the subject-to versus assumption distinction are where the exam spends its questions, so write those two out from memory once after reading.
