Chapter 13 — Types of Mortgages and Sources of Financing

What this chapter is. Chapter 12 was the legal skeleton of any mortgage. This chapter is the catalog: how loans are repaid, which loans are backed by the government and which are not, the specialty loans that solve particular problems, and who actually supplies the money. The exam cares because a licensee is expected to recognize the right tool for a buyer's situation, and because each loan type has two or three defining features that the questions turn on. Learn the defining features, not the marketing.

Repayment plans

How the principal comes back to the lender defines the loan.

A fully amortized loan is paid in equal installments, each covering that month's interest plus a slice of principal, so the balance reaches zero on the last payment. Early on, most of each payment is interest; near the end, most is principal. The payment never changes on a fixed-rate amortized loan; only the split changes.

A term loan, also called a straight or interest-only loan, pays only interest during the term, with the entire principal due at the end. The balance never goes down until the last day.

A balloon mortgage (partially amortized) has payments that pay down some principal, but not enough, so a large lump sum is due at maturity. Common in seller financing.

A graduated payment mortgage starts with low payments that rise on a schedule. In the early years the payment may not cover the interest, which produces negative amortization: the unpaid interest is added to the balance, so the borrower owes more than they borrowed.

A biweekly mortgage takes half a monthly payment every two weeks. Twenty-six half payments make thirteen full payments a year, so the loan pays off faster.

The scene: a seller on Jacaranda Way carries a $90,000 note for the buyer, payments figured as if over 30 years, with the whole remaining balance due in year five. That is a balloon. The buyer's plan has to be a refinance before the balloon date.

The trap: negative amortization. A payment that does not cover the interest does not reduce the balance; it increases it. The exam will describe it without naming it.

Fixed rate and adjustable rate

A fixed-rate mortgage keeps one interest rate for the life of the loan.

An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) has a rate that moves with an outside measure. The parts, by name:

  • Index: the outside benchmark the rate follows. The lender does not control it.
  • Margin: the lender's fixed markup added to the index. It does not change during the loan.
  • Index + margin = the fully indexed rate.
  • Adjustment period: how often the rate resets.
  • Caps: limits on how much the rate can move at each adjustment (periodic cap) and over the life of the loan (lifetime cap). A payment cap limits the payment instead of the rate, and can cause negative amortization if the rate rises faster than the payment is allowed to.
  • A teaser rate is an initial rate set below the fully indexed rate to make the first payments look low.

The scene: a buyer on Pelican Point Road takes an ARM with an index currently at 4.25 percent and a margin of 2.5 percent. The fully indexed rate is 6.75 percent. If the index rises to 5 percent at the next adjustment, the rate becomes 7.5 percent, subject to the caps.

The trap: which part changes. The index moves. The margin stays put. An answer that has the lender "raising the margin" is wrong.

Conventional loans and private mortgage insurance

A conventional loan is any loan not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. The lender carries the risk itself.

Because the lender is exposed, a conventional loan with a high loan-to-value ratio, meaning a small down payment, usually requires private mortgage insurance (PMI), which protects the lender (not the borrower) against loss on default. The customary dividing line is a loan above 80 percent of the property's value.

The federal Homeowners Protection Act sets when PMI on most conventional loans has to come off. The borrower can request cancellation when the balance reaches 80 percent of the original value, if the payment history is good. It terminates automatically at 78 percent of the original value.

Conventional loans that meet the size and underwriting standards of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are conforming loans. Loans above the conforming size limit are jumbo (nonconforming) loans. The conforming limit is set each year and is higher in some high-cost areas; take the current figure from the textbook.

The scene: a buyer on Royal Poinciana Drive puts 10 percent down on a conventional loan. She pays PMI from day one. When her balance amortizes to 78 percent of the original value, it drops off without her asking.

The trap: PMI protects the lender. Every answer that says it protects the borrower if the borrower loses a job, or pays off the loan if the borrower dies, is describing something else (credit life or mortgage life insurance).

FHA-insured loans

The Federal Housing Administration, part of HUD, does not lend money. It insures loans made by approved lenders, so the lender is repaid if the borrower defaults.

Defining features:

  • Low down payment. Under current FHA rules the minimum is 3.5 percent of the price for a borrower who meets the program's credit-score standard. The book may describe the minimum investment differently; use its version.
  • Mortgage insurance premium (MIP). The borrower pays both an up-front premium at closing (which can be financed into the loan) and an annual premium paid monthly. Take the current percentages from the textbook.
  • Owner occupancy. FHA loans are for the borrower's own residence.
  • No prepayment penalty.
  • Assumable by a buyer who qualifies.
  • Loan limits vary by county, so the maximum in Miami-Dade is not the maximum in a rural Panhandle county.
  • The property has to meet FHA's minimum property standards, checked by an FHA appraisal.

The scene: a first-time buyer on Osprey Lane with modest savings and solid income qualifies for an FHA loan on a $250,000 townhouse. At 3.5 percent, the down payment is $8,750. The up-front premium is added to the loan.

The trap: "FHA loans the money." FHA insures; a bank or mortgage company lends. And FHA's insurance premium is called MIP, while the conventional version is PMI. The exam uses the abbreviations to test whether the program is clear.

VA-guaranteed loans

The Department of Veterans Affairs guarantees a portion of a loan made by an approved lender to an eligible veteran, active-duty servicemember, or certain surviving spouses.

Defining features:

  • No down payment required, as long as the price does not exceed the VA's appraised value and the veteran has full entitlement.
  • Certificate of Eligibility: the document proving the veteran qualifies.
  • Certificate of Reasonable Value (CRV): the VA appraisal. If the contract price exceeds it, the veteran can pay the difference in cash but cannot borrow it.
  • Funding fee paid by the veteran, which can be financed; certain disabled veterans are exempt. Take the rates from the textbook.
  • No PMI.
  • Owner occupancy required.
  • Assumable. If a non-veteran assumes it, the original veteran's entitlement stays tied up in that loan unless a qualified veteran buyer substitutes their own entitlement. The veteran should also get a release of liability from the VA.

The scene: a Navy veteran stationed in Jacksonville buys a house on Lantana Avenue with no money down. Later he sells to a civilian who assumes the VA loan. Unless the VA releases him, he is still liable, and his entitlement is still committed to that house.

The trap: "insured" versus "guaranteed." FHA insures. VA guarantees. And the VA guaranty protects the lender, not the veteran.

Purchase-money mortgages and seller financing

A purchase-money mortgage is any mortgage given to finance the purchase of the property itself. In exam usage it most often means the seller financing part of the price: the buyer gives the seller a note and mortgage instead of cash.

Why a seller would do it: to sell a property that lenders will not finance, to earn interest, or to spread out the tax on a gain. Why a buyer would: easier qualifying and fewer lender costs.

Florida-specific: a seller-held note and mortgage on Florida property carry the same Florida taxes as a bank loan: documentary stamp tax on the note and intangible tax on the mortgage. Seller financing does not escape them.

The scene: an owner of a duplex on Hibiscus Court sells for $400,000, takes $80,000 down and holds a $320,000 purchase-money mortgage. The note and mortgage get stamped and taxed at recording exactly as a bank's would.

The trap: assuming "purchase-money" only means a seller loan. A bank loan used to buy the house is also a purchase-money mortgage. The phrase describes what the money buys, not who lends it.

The specialty mortgages

Each one exists to solve one problem. Learn the problem.

  • Blanket mortgage: one mortgage covering two or more parcels. Used by developers. Paired with a partial release clause so individual lots can be sold free of the lien.
  • Package mortgage: one mortgage covering real and personal property together, such as a condo sold furnished with appliances and furniture.
  • Open-end mortgage: lets the borrower borrow more later, up to a stated amount, under the same mortgage, without a new loan.
  • Construction loan: short-term, interim financing paid out in draws as the building is completed, then replaced by permanent financing (the takeout loan).
  • Wraparound mortgage: the seller keeps the existing loan in place and gives the buyer a new, larger loan that "wraps around" it. The buyer pays the seller; the seller keeps paying the original lender. Works only if the original loan has no enforceable alienation clause, or the lender consents.
  • Budget mortgage: monthly payment covers principal and interest plus a share of taxes and insurance (the escrow, or PITI). Most residential loans today.
  • Reverse mortgage: for homeowners aged 62 or older. The lender pays the homeowner, in a lump sum, monthly or as a line of credit, against the home's equity, and the balance grows instead of shrinking. It comes due when the owner dies, sells, or permanently moves out. The federally insured version is the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM).
  • Shared-appreciation mortgage: the lender gives a below-market rate in return for a share of the property's future appreciation.
  • Home equity loan or line of credit: a junior mortgage against equity the owner already has.

The scene: a retired couple on Magnolia Bluff own their house outright and need income. A reverse mortgage pays them monthly. They make no payments; the balance is repaid from the sale after they leave the home.

The trap: blanket versus package. Blanket covers more than one property. Package covers more than one kind of property (real plus personal) on a single parcel. The exam swaps them.

The primary mortgage market

The primary mortgage market is where loans are made directly to borrowers. The sources the chapter lists:

  • Commercial banks: historically short-term and commercial lenders; now major residential lenders too.
  • Savings associations (thrifts): historically the core residential lender.
  • Credit unions: member-owned; lend to members.
  • Mortgage bankers (mortgage companies): lend their own or borrowed money, usually sell the loans on the secondary market, and often keep servicing them, collecting payments for a fee.
  • Mortgage brokers: do not lend. They bring borrower and lender together for a fee. In Florida they are licensed by the Office of Financial Regulation, not by FREC.
  • Life insurance companies: large, long-term lenders, mainly on big commercial properties.
  • Private and seller financing.

The trap: mortgage banker versus mortgage broker. The banker lends and services. The broker arranges and is paid for arranging.

The secondary mortgage market

The secondary mortgage market is where existing loans are bought and sold. Lenders sell the loans they made, get cash back, and lend it again. Without it, a local bank could only lend as much as its depositors had deposited.

The three names:

  • Fannie Mae (Federal National Mortgage Association): buys conventional loans (and historically FHA and VA loans), packages them into mortgage-backed securities.
  • Freddie Mac (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation): created to buy loans from savings associations; now buys conventional loans broadly. Fannie and Freddie are government-sponsored enterprises, under federal conservatorship since 2008.
  • Ginnie Mae (Government National Mortgage Association): part of HUD. It does not buy loans. It guarantees securities backed by pools of FHA and VA loans.

Why this matters to a licensee: the secondary market's standards are why lenders across the country ask for the same documents, use the same forms, and apply the same ratios. A conventional loan that does not meet Fannie and Freddie's standards is harder to sell, so it is harder to get.

The trap: Ginnie Mae. It is the government one, and it guarantees securities of government-backed loans. It does not buy conventional loans.

Qualifying the borrower

Underwriting is the lender's decision whether to make the loan. It weighs the borrower's capacity (income and debts), credit (history and score), capital (savings and down payment) and the collateral (the property, through the appraisal).

Capacity is measured with two ratios, both against gross monthly income:

  • Housing expense ratio (front-end): the monthly principal, interest, taxes and insurance (PITI), plus HOA dues if any, divided by gross monthly income.
  • Total obligations ratio (back-end): PITI plus all other recurring monthly debts (car loan, student loans, minimum credit-card payments), divided by gross monthly income.

The textbook ratios are commonly 28 percent and 36 percent for conventional loans and 31 percent and 43 percent for FHA. VA looks mainly at the total ratio and at residual income. Use the book's figures if they differ.

Worked scene: a buyer on Pelican Point Road earns $96,000 a year, or $8,000 a month. At 28 percent, the most PITI the conventional guideline allows is $2,240. At 36 percent, total monthly debts including PITI cannot exceed $2,880. If she has a $450 car payment and $150 in card minimums, that is $600 of other debt, so the PITI limit from the back end is $2,880 − $600 = $2,280. The lower of the two answers governs: $2,240.

The trap: using net (take-home) income. The ratios use gross income. And the exam expects the lower of the two results, not the higher.

Points and loan costs

A point is 1 percent of the loan amount, not of the price. Two kinds:

  • Discount points are prepaid interest. The borrower pays them at closing to buy a lower interest rate, which raises the lender's effective yield.
  • Origination fee: the lender's charge for making the loan, often expressed in points too.

Worked scene: a buyer on Osprey Lane borrows $300,000 on a $375,000 house and pays 2 discount points. The cost is $300,000 × 0.02 = $6,000. Figured on the price by mistake, it would be $7,500, which is the wrong answer the exam will offer.

Who may pay points: under most programs the seller can pay some or all of the buyer's points as a concession, within the program's limits; take those limits from the textbook.

The trap: points on the price. Always on the loan.

How to use this chapter

Read the government-loan sections (FHA, VA) and the specialty mortgages here first, since those are the defining-feature questions; then open the chapter. Work the qualifying-ratio and points examples by hand once, because Chapter 14 builds on both.
