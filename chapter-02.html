Study companion — Chapter 2: License Law and Qualifications for Licensure

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course, Chapter 2. Pre-reading: go through a section here, then open that part of the chapter, so the book reads as a second pass instead of a cold start.

What this chapter is. Who has to hold a Florida real estate license, who does not, and what it takes to get one and keep it. It answers three questions in order: what activity the law is aimed at, which people doing that activity are left alone, and what a person has to do — education, application, background check, exam, and then more education — to be allowed to do it. The exam cares because this is where the definitions live. Almost every later violation question assumes you can tell whether the person in the fact pattern needed a license in the first place, and the wrong answers are built from people who look like they are exempt and are not.

Everything below is Florida law: Chapter 475 of the Florida Statutes and the FREC rules in Chapter 61J2 of the Florida Administrative Code. Where a number is stated, it is certain. Where it says "under Florida law" without a number, take the number from the textbook. This chapter is dense with ages, hours and timeframes, and the textbook's figures are the ones to memorize.

Why Florida regulates real estate

A real estate transaction is usually the largest purchase a person makes, it is made rarely, and the buyer or seller typically knows far less than the professional across the table. That combination — big money, rare transactions, unequal knowledge — is the classic case for licensing.

Florida's answer is Chapter 475, Part I, which governs brokers, broker associates and sales associates. (Part II governs appraisers, a separate profession with its own board; see Chapter 16.) The law is administered through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR), and the Florida Real Estate Commission (FREC) sits within DBPR's Division of Real Estate. FREC makes the rules and decides discipline; DBPR handles the administrative side — applications, investigations, records.

The point to hold onto is the stated purpose. Florida licenses real estate practitioners to protect the public, not to protect the profession or to keep the number of agents down. The state does this under its police power — the same power behind zoning and building codes — which lets it regulate an occupation for public health, safety and welfare. A license is a privilege the state grants and can take back, not a right.

The trap: an answer choice that says the license law exists to "maintain high commission rates," "limit competition," or "protect licensees from unqualified competitors." Each sounds plausible. The purpose is protection of the public, and every rule in the chapter is justified that way.

What counts as real estate services

Chapter 475 defines a broker by what the person does, not by the title on the business card. A person is acting as a broker when, for another and for compensation or valuable consideration (paid, promised, or expected), they do any of the following with real property, an interest in real property, or a business enterprise or business opportunity:

  • sell, buy, exchange, rent or lease it, or negotiate any of those;
  • appraise it;
  • auction it;
  • advertise it, or hold themselves out to the public as being in that business;
  • take any part in procuring buyers, sellers, landlords or tenants, or in the negotiation or closing of a deal.

It also covers people who advertise rental property information or rental lists for a fee.

Two features of the definition do most of the work on the exam. First, the activity list is wide. It includes offering, attempting or agreeing to do any of these things — a deal does not have to close. Second, it reaches business opportunities, not only land and buildings. A person who, for a fee, finds a buyer for a dry cleaner on Strangler Fig Avenue is performing real estate services even if the building is leased and no real property changes hands.

On appraisal: the definition includes it, which is why a licensee may give opinions of value. The limit, covered in Chapter 16, is that a licensee may never call that opinion an appraisal.

The trap: "the deal never closed, so no license was needed." Florida's definition covers attempting and offering. A person who shows a house on Pond Apple Road to three prospects for a promised fee, and never gets an offer, has still performed real estate services.

The compensation element

The definition only bites when the person acts for compensation, and Florida reads "compensation" broadly. It is not limited to a commission check. Valuable consideration includes anything of value: a gift card, free rent, a used car, a share of the profit, a promised favor. It counts whether it is paid directly or indirectly, before or after, and even if it is only expected.

The other half is "for another." An owner selling their own property is not acting for another, which is why owners are exempt (next section).

Scene: a homeowner on Wax Myrtle Lane tells a neighbor, "If you find someone to buy my house, I'll give you my boat." The neighbor brings a buyer; the boat (a scenario value of $15,000) changes hands. The neighbor acted for another, for valuable consideration, and procured a buyer. That is unlicensed brokerage.

Florida also does not carve out an occasional or one-time transaction. A single deal for compensation is enough.

The trap: "it was only one sale" or "I didn't take money, I took a boat." Both fail. One transaction counts, and a boat is valuable consideration. The flip side is equally tested: a friend who helps for free, with no promise or expectation of anything, is not acting for compensation and needs no license.

Who is exempt

Chapter 475 lists people who perform acts that look like brokerage but do not need a license. The exam's job is to test where each exemption stops. The ones you will see most:

  • Owners dealing with their own property. An owner selling, leasing or renting property they own is not acting for another. This extends to a business entity dealing in its own property through its regular officers or employees, within the textbook's limits.
  • Attorneys at law acting within the scope of their duties as attorneys. A Florida attorney who drafts the contract and handles the closing for a client is practicing law. The exemption covers legal work, not running a brokerage on the side.
  • Attorneys in fact acting under a power of attorney, but only to sign contracts or conveyances on the owner's behalf. The power of attorney lets the agent execute documents. It does not let the agent go find buyers for a fee.
  • Court-appointed and other fiduciaries — a personal representative of an estate, a receiver, a trustee in bankruptcy, a guardian, or a trustee acting under a trust — when acting under court order or under the authority of the will or trust.
  • Salaried employees of an owner of an apartment community who work in the community's on-site rental office in a leasing capacity. The textbook gives the exact conditions on how they may be paid; take them from there.
  • People selling cemetery lots.

The textbook's full list is longer. Learn the shape of each exemption from its reason: the owner is not acting for another; the attorney is practicing law; the fiduciary is acting under a court's or a document's authority; the apartment employee is a salaried worker in a narrow leasing role on the owner's own property.

Scene: a son holds a power of attorney for his mother, who owns a rental house on Beautyberry Court. He signs the listing agreement and later the deed on her behalf. Exempt. Then a neighbor asks him to find a buyer for her own condo and offers a fee. The power of attorney covers his mother's documents, not the neighbor's sale, and not finding buyers for anyone. That second job needs a license.

The trap: stretching an exemption past its reason. The apartment leasing employee who also starts showing units at a different complex owned by someone else; the attorney who takes a "finder's fee" for introducing a buyer when there was no legal work; the personal representative who starts selling property for other families. Each one stepped outside the activity that justified the exemption.

The license categories

Florida issues three kinds of real estate license to individuals:

  • Sales associate. Performs real estate services, but only under the direction, control and management of an employing broker (or an owner-developer, where the textbook covers that case). A sales associate cannot work independently, cannot hold escrow money in their own name, and can be paid for brokerage work only by their employing broker.
  • Broker. May operate a brokerage, employ associates, hold escrow deposits, and be paid directly by the public. The broker is the person legally accountable for the brokerage.
  • Broker associate. A person who holds a broker's license but chooses to work under another broker, the way a sales associate does. The extra qualification does not change who is in charge.

A sales associate or broker associate works for one employing broker at a time. A license is issued to a natural person; business entities register with the state rather than holding a license of their own, a topic the next chapter picks up.

Why Florida does it this way: the public needs one accountable person behind every transaction. The tiered license lets newcomers practice while a more experienced, more heavily qualified broker answers for their work.

Scene: a sales associate at a brokerage on Strangler Fig Avenue is offered a direct "bonus" by a grateful seller. She has to decline it, or have it paid through her broker. Her license lets her do the work only as her broker's associate.

The trap: assuming "broker associate" is a separate, lower license or a sales associate with a fancier title. It is a full broker's license held by someone who is working under another broker.

Qualifying for a sales associate license

The core requirements under Florida law:

  • Age. At least 18 years old.
  • Education. A high school diploma or its equivalent, plus the FREC-approved pre-license course for sales associates — the 63-hour course this library is built around — completed with a passing grade on the end-of-course exam. Take the passing grade for the course exam from the textbook. Florida exempts certain applicants with qualifying college education in real estate from the pre-license course; take the specifics from the book.
  • Character. The applicant must be honest, truthful, trustworthy and of good character, and have a reputation for fair dealing. This is a real requirement, not a formality, and it is the reason for the background check.
  • The state exam, covered below.

Florida law also sets how long a completed pre-license course stays good for qualifying for the exam. Take that timeframe from the textbook.

Scene: a recent graduate living on Pond Apple Road finishes the 63-hour course a month after turning 18. She meets the age and education requirements. Whether she gets a license now depends on the application, the background check and the exam.

The trap: confusing the course exam with the state exam. Passing the end-of-course exam makes you eligible; it does not license you. The state exam is a separate test given by the state's testing vendor.

The application, fingerprints and disclosure

The applicant files an application with DBPR and pays the required fees. As part of it, the applicant submits fingerprints electronically through an approved vendor so the state can run a criminal background check through state and federal databases. Take the timing rules and the period an application stays valid from the textbook.

The application asks about the applicant's background, including criminal history and any discipline on a professional license elsewhere. The rule the exam cares about is disclosure: the applicant must answer truthfully. Under Florida law the question reaches more than convictions — a plea of guilty or of no contest (nolo contendere), and a case where adjudication was withheld, still have to be disclosed. Take from the textbook exactly which minor offenses are excluded.

Why Florida does it this way: a criminal record does not automatically bar a license. FREC looks at the nature of the offense, how long ago it was, and evidence of rehabilitation. What the Commission will not forgive easily is lying about it, because concealment on the application is itself evidence that the applicant is not honest and trustworthy — the very thing the character requirement tests. A license obtained by fraud or concealment can be denied, and if it was already issued, revoked.

Scene: an applicant on Wax Myrtle Lane pled no contest to a misdemeanor eight years ago and the judge withheld adjudication. He leaves the question blank because "I was never convicted." The background check finds it. The old case might have been approved on its own; the omission is the problem.

The trap: "adjudication was withheld, so there is nothing to disclose." On the Florida application, there is. And the other version: "any criminal record disqualifies the applicant." It does not; FREC weighs it, and the applicant has the right to be heard if the application is denied.

The state licensing exam

After the application is approved, the applicant is eligible to take the state exam. The sales associate exam has 100 questions, and the passing score is 75.

An applicant who fails can retake it; take any retake rules and the eligibility window from the textbook.

Once the applicant passes, the license is issued. A sales associate's license can be active only when it is tied to an employing broker. A new licensee without a broker holds a license but cannot practice.

Scene: a new licensee passes on a Tuesday, celebrates, and on Wednesday shows a friend's listing on Beautyberry Court before any broker has registered her as an associate. She holds a license, but she is not working under a broker, and she had no right to perform services that day.

The trap: treating "passed the exam" as "may now practice." Practice requires an active license under an employing broker.

Post-licensing education

A new license comes with a condition. Before the first renewal, a sales associate must complete 45 hours of FREC-approved post-licensing education and pass its end-of-course exam. A new broker's post-licensing requirement is 60 hours.

If a sales associate does not complete the post-licensing course before the first renewal, the license becomes null and void. It does not go inactive or get suspended; it ceases to exist. Under Florida law, getting back into the business means going back through the licensing requirements; take the exact path, and any hardship exceptions, from the textbook.

The 45-hour course replaces continuing education for that first renewal. The licensee does not also owe the regular continuing education hours that cycle.

Why Florida does it this way: the 63-hour course is enough to pass an exam, not to handle a career, so the first renewal is made conditional on follow-through.

Scene: a sales associate at an office on Pond Apple Road has a strong first year and keeps putting off the post-licensing course. The first renewal deadline passes. Her closings next week now involve a person who holds no license.

The trap: "void" versus "inactive." An inactive license can be reactivated by meeting the requirements. A void license is gone. The exam also swaps the hour counts — 45 for a sales associate, 60 for a broker.

Continuing education

After the first renewal, each two-year renewal cycle requires 14 hours of FREC-approved continuing education, and those hours include a required Core Law component. Take the hours of Core Law and the other required topics in the 14 from the textbook, and take the renewal deadlines from the textbook too.

Why Florida does it this way: Chapter 475 and the FREC rules change. Core Law exists so that every licensee, every cycle, hears what changed.

Scene: a broker associate on Strangler Fig Avenue completed post-licensing two years ago. For this renewal she needs the 14 hours, with Core Law included. Last cycle's post-licensing course does not count toward this cycle.

The trap: the sequence. First renewal: post-licensing, no regular CE. Every renewal after that: 14 hours of CE. The exam offers "14 hours plus post-licensing at the first renewal" and "14 hours starting with the first renewal." Both are wrong.

Nonresidents and mutual recognition

Florida does not require a person to live in Florida to hold a Florida license. A nonresident may qualify the same way a resident does — course, application, background check, exam — and Florida law defines who counts as a resident; take that definition, and the notice a licensee owes when they become a Florida resident, from the textbook.

Mutual recognition is a shortcut for nonresidents from certain other states. Florida has agreements with a set of states under which a licensee from one of them who is not a Florida resident can skip Florida's pre-license course and the full state exam. Instead, the applicant passes a shorter exam on Florida real estate law. The applicant still has to meet Florida's other requirements, including the character requirement and the background check.

The list of states with agreements changes, and so do the details of the Florida law exam. Take both from the textbook.

Why Florida does it this way: the national material — contracts, finance, appraisal concepts — is largely the same everywhere, so a recognized license from a comparable state covers it. Florida's own license law is not the same anywhere else, so that part is always tested.

Scene: a broker licensed in another state lives there and wants to list a client's vacation condo on Beautyberry Court. If that state is one with a mutual recognition agreement, she takes the Florida law exam rather than the full course and exam. If it is not, she qualifies the regular way. Either way, she needs a Florida license to do the work in Florida.

The trap: "mutual recognition means no exam." There is still an exam, on Florida law. And mutual recognition is for nonresidents; it is not a back door for someone who already lives in Florida.

Unlicensed activity

Performing real estate services for compensation without a valid, active license is a crime in Florida: it is a third-degree felony. The textbook gives the maximum prison term and fine for that degree. DBPR can also act administratively against unlicensed people, including cease-and-desist orders and fines; take the amounts from the textbook.

Two consequences follow beyond the criminal charge. First, under Florida law an unlicensed person cannot sue to collect a commission for real estate services; the deal does not create an enforceable right to be paid. Second, a licensee who pays an unlicensed person for real estate services is also committing a violation. Florida allows narrow exceptions, such as a referral fee paid to a broker licensed in another state who does not perform services in Florida; take the conditions from the textbook.

Scene: a licensed sales associate on Wax Myrtle Lane lets her unlicensed brother-in-law show listings on weekends and splits her commission with him. He is guilty of unlicensed brokerage, a felony. She is guilty of a license-law violation for paying him, and her broker has a supervision problem. If she stops paying him, he cannot sue her for his share.

Unlicensed assistants are allowed to do clerical work — scheduling, filing, data entry — for a salary or hourly wage, never per transaction. The line is crossed when the assistant negotiates, shows property as a salesperson, or is paid based on deals closing.

The trap: the level of the offense. The exam offers "second-degree misdemeanor" because most license-law violations are misdemeanors. Unlicensed practice is the exception, and it is a felony. The other trap: the license that is void or inactive. A person whose license is void because post-licensing was missed, or who works while inactive, is performing services without a valid active license.

How to use this chapter

Read the sections in order, then work the chapter in the course. The first five sections are definitions, and the exam tests them as fact patterns: for each scene above, ask the three questions the chapter asks — was it for another, was it for compensation, does an exemption actually reach it. The rest of the chapter is numbers. Write down every age, hour count, score and timeframe the textbook gives, especially the ones this companion left to the book. If a number in the textbook differs from anything here, the textbook wins.
