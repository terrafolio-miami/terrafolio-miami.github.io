Study companion — Chapter 16: Real Estate Appraisal

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course, Chapter 16. Pre-reading: go through a section here, then open that part of the chapter, so the book reads as a second pass instead of a cold start.

What this chapter is. How value is estimated, who is allowed to call that estimate an appraisal, and the three methods appraisers use to get there. The exam cares for two reasons. First, there is a licensing line in Florida that a sales associate can cross without noticing, and crossing it is a violation. Second, this is one of the few chapters with real arithmetic, and the state exam always includes a few value problems — a cap rate, a gross rent multiplier, a depreciation figure, a comparable adjustment. The math is simple once the setup is clear. The setup is where people lose points.

Everything below is Florida law and Florida practice. Where a number is stated, it is certain. Where it is not, it says "under Florida law" — take the number from the textbook.

Who may appraise in Florida

Florida licenses appraisers separately from real estate brokers and sales associates. Appraiser licensing lives in Chapter 475, Part II, of the Florida Statutes, and appraisers are regulated by their own board, the Florida Real Estate Appraisal Board, not by FREC. There are levels — registered trainee, licensed, certified residential, and certified general — and each level may handle different kinds and sizes of assignments. Take the scope of each level from the textbook.

A sales associate is not an appraiser. But a sales associate gives opinions of value all the time, and Florida law allows it. Section 475.612 says that a broker or sales associate who, in the ordinary course of business, performs a comparative market analysis, gives a price opinion, or gives an opinion of the value of real estate does not need an appraiser's license — but that opinion may never be referred to or construed as an appraisal. The same section lets licensees provide valuation services for compensation, as long as they do not represent themselves as certified, licensed or registered appraisers.

Two related terms:

  • A comparative market analysis (CMA) is the licensee's estimate of what a property will likely sell for, built from recent sales, current listings and expired listings nearby. It is a pricing tool for a listing presentation or an offer.
  • A broker price opinion (BPO) is a similar estimate usually done for a lender, servicer or investor, often for a fee. Florida treats it the same way: it is a price opinion, not an appraisal.

Why Florida draws the line: an appraisal is relied on by lenders and federal agencies as an independent, standards-bound opinion. Appraisals are done under USPAP — the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice — and federal law (FIRREA) requires a state-licensed or certified appraiser for federally related transactions. A licensee who is paid on commission has a stake in the price, which is the opposite of independence.

The trap: a sales associate on Lignumvitae Street prepares a careful 20-page CMA for a seller's estate attorney and titles it "Appraisal Report." The work may be excellent. The title is the violation. The other version: "a sales associate may not charge for a BPO." Wrong — a licensee may be paid for valuation services, as long as the fee is paid through the employing broker and the work is not called an appraisal.

Value, price and cost

Three words the exam treats as different things.

Market value is the most probable price a property should bring in a competitive, open market, with a willing buyer and willing seller, both reasonably informed, neither under undue pressure, with reasonable exposure time, and payment in cash or its equivalent. It is an estimate of what should happen.

Market price (or sales price) is what actually happened — what a buyer actually paid. A sale between relatives, a foreclosure sale, or a sale with unusual seller financing can produce a price far from market value.

Cost is what it takes to build the improvement. A homeowner on Mahogany Drive who spends $90,000 on a backyard waterpark has spent the cost. Whether the market will pay any of that back is a value question.

Other kinds of value appear in the chapter — insurable value, assessed value, investment value (value to one particular investor), value in use, and so on. The appraiser's job is almost always market value.

The trap: an answer that says a recent sale "proves" market value. A sale is evidence of value, not the value itself. If the buyer was the seller's brother, the sale is not an arm's-length transaction and an appraiser would likely reject it as a comparable.

Characteristics of value

For something to have value, four things must be present. The usual memory aid is DUST:

  • Demand — people want it and can pay for it.
  • Utility — it is useful; it can do something.
  • Scarcity — there is a limited supply of it.
  • Transferability — ownership can pass from one person to another.

Air has utility and demand but no scarcity, so it has no market value. A beautiful house on land that legally cannot be conveyed has no transferability.

The trap: "cost" is not one of the four. Something can cost a lot to make and still have little value if no one wants it.

Forces that affect value

The chapter groups outside influences into four forces. The exam asks which category a given fact falls into.

  • Physical (environmental): location, topography, soil, climate, access, flood exposure, view.
  • Economic: employment, interest rates, availability of mortgage money, local business growth.
  • Political (governmental): zoning, building codes, taxes, environmental rules, rent controls.
  • Social: population trends, household size, attitudes toward schools, crime, lifestyle preferences.

The trap: zoning feels physical because it is about land. It is political — it comes from government. Interest rates feel political because the Federal Reserve sets policy. In this chapter they are economic.

Principles of value

These are the rules of thumb behind every valuation. The ones the exam uses most:

Highest and best use. The use that is legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive — the use that produces the highest value. A one-story house on a lot zoned for a five-story medical office may have a highest and best use as a medical office site, and the land value reflects that even while the house stands.

Substitution. A buyer will not pay more for a property than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. This principle is the foundation of all three approaches.

Conformity. Value is maintained when a property is reasonably similar to its neighbors.

Regression and progression. The best house in a modest neighborhood is pulled down toward its neighbors (regression). The modest house in an expensive neighborhood is pulled up (progression). A four-bedroom pool home among two-bedroom cottages is the regression example.

Contribution. An improvement is worth what it adds to market value, not what it cost. A $60,000 pool that adds $25,000 in value contributed $25,000.

Anticipation. Value is the present worth of future benefits. This is the principle behind the income approach.

Supply and demand, competition, change. Values rise when demand exceeds supply; excess profits attract competitors; neighborhoods move through growth, stability, decline and sometimes revitalization.

Plottage and assemblage. Assemblage is the act of combining adjoining parcels. Plottage is the extra value created when the combined parcel is worth more than the parts. Two 50-foot lots on Lignumvitae Street worth $150,000 each may be worth $360,000 together if the combined lot allows a development neither could support alone. The $60,000 increase is the plottage value.

The trap: assemblage and plottage swapped. Assemblage is the process; plottage is the increment in value.

The appraisal process

An appraisal follows a fixed sequence:

  1. Define the problem — identify the property, the property rights, the purpose and intended use, the effective date, and the type of value sought.
  2. Determine the scope of work.
  3. Gather and analyze data — general data (region, city, neighborhood) and specific data (the site, the improvements, the comparables).
  4. Estimate highest and best use.
  5. Estimate land value.
  6. Apply the approaches to value.
  7. Reconcile the results into a single opinion.
  8. Report the final opinion of value.

The trap: reconciliation is not averaging. If an appraiser gets $410,000 from sales comparison, $425,000 from cost and $395,000 from income on a single-family home, the answer is not $410,000 because it is the average. The appraiser weighs each approach by how reliable it is for this property type. For an owner-occupied house, sales comparison usually carries the most weight.

The sales comparison approach

The approach that matches how buyers think: start from what similar properties actually sold for. The appraiser picks recent, nearby, similar sales (comparables, or comps), then adjusts each comp's price to account for its differences from the subject.

The rule that matters: adjust the comparable, never the subject. The aim every time is the price this comp would have brought if it were exactly like the subject.

  • If the comp is better than the subject on a feature, subtract the value of that feature from the comp's price.
  • If the comp is inferior, add.

Memory aid: CBS — Comp Better, Subtract. CIA — Comp Inferior, Add.

Worked example. Subject: 2214 Lignumvitae Street, three bedrooms, two baths, no pool, one-car garage. Scenario adjustment values from the appraiser's market data: pool $20,000; extra bathroom $10,000; two-car garage instead of one $8,000.

Comp A sold for $430,000. Three bedrooms, two baths, has a pool, one-car garage. - The comp has a pool the subject lacks. Comp better: subtract $20,000. - Adjusted price: $430,000 − $20,000 = $410,000.

Comp B sold for $395,000. Three bedrooms, one bath, no pool, two-car garage. - One fewer bath than the subject. Comp inferior: add $10,000. - Two-car garage, better than the subject's one-car. Comp better: subtract $8,000. - Adjusted price: $395,000 + $10,000 − $8,000 = $397,000.

Comp C sold for $402,000. Same features as the subject. - No adjustments: $402,000.

The adjusted range is $397,000 to $410,000. Comp C needed no adjustment, so it is usually the most reliable, and the appraiser would weight it most heavily — something near $402,000 is a defensible indication. Again: weighting, not averaging.

Typical adjustment categories: date of sale (market conditions), location, physical features, financing terms, and conditions of sale.

The trap: adjusting the subject. A question that says "the subject has a pool, so add $20,000 to the subject" is built to catch that move. The subject is the unknown. Only comps get adjusted.

The cost approach

Used most for new construction, special-purpose properties that rarely sell (a church, a school, a fire station), and insurance purposes. The logic is substitution: a buyer would not pay more than it costs to buy land and build an equivalent building.

The formula:

Cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation + land value = value

Land is valued separately, as if vacant, usually by sales comparison. Land does not depreciate.

Reproduction cost is the cost to build an exact replica, with the same materials and design. Replacement cost is the cost to build a building with the same utility using current materials and methods. A 1920s house with hand-carved cypress trim has a reproduction cost far above its replacement cost.

Cost-estimating methods, from least to most detailed: square-foot method (the most common), unit-in-place (cost per component — roof, walls, foundation), and quantity survey (every material and labor item, the way a builder bids).

Depreciation is any loss in value from any cause. Three kinds:

  • Physical deterioration — wear and tear: a leaking roof, worn carpet, peeling paint. Can be curable or incurable.
  • Functional obsolescence — a design problem inside the property: a bedroom reached only through another bedroom, one bathroom in a five-bedroom house, no central air in Miami. Can be curable or incurable.
  • External (economic) obsolescence — a problem outside the property line: a new landfill, a highway interchange next door, a neighborhood in decline. Always incurable, because the owner cannot fix what they do not own.

Worked example, straight-line (age-life) depreciation. A house at 618 Mahogany Drive:

  • Living area: 2,000 square feet.
  • Current replacement cost: $180 per square foot (scenario figure).
  • Effective age: 10 years. Total economic life: 50 years.
  • Land value, from vacant lot sales: $90,000.

Step 1 — cost new: 2,000 × $180 = $360,000.

Step 2 — depreciation rate: effective age ÷ economic life = 10 ÷ 50 = 20 percent (2 percent per year).

Step 3 — accrued depreciation: $360,000 × 0.20 = $72,000.

Step 4 — depreciated cost: $360,000 − $72,000 = $288,000.

Step 5 — add land: $288,000 + $90,000 = $378,000.

Effective age is not the same as actual age. A 30-year-old house that was fully renovated might have an effective age of 10. The calculation uses effective age.

The trap: depreciating the land. If the question gives a total property cost and asks for depreciation, take the land out first, depreciate only the building, then add the land back.

The income approach

Used for property bought for the income it produces: apartments, offices, retail, warehouses. The principle is anticipation — value is the present worth of future income.

The build-up, in order:

  1. Potential gross income (PGI) — all units rented all year at market rent.
  2. Minus vacancy and collection loss.
  3. Plus other income (laundry, parking, vending).
  4. Equals effective gross income (EGI).
  5. Minus operating expenses — taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, utilities the owner pays, reserves for replacement.
  6. Equals net operating income (NOI).

Then: Value = NOI ÷ capitalization rate.

The memory aid is IRV: Income = Rate × Value. Cover the one being asked for. Value = I ÷ R. Rate = I ÷ V. Income = R × V.

What does NOT go into operating expenses: mortgage payments (debt service), depreciation, and the owner's income taxes. Those are personal to the investor, not to the property. The exam lists them as distractors.

Worked example. An eight-unit apartment building at 3307 Lignumvitae Street.

  • Rent: $1,500 per unit per month (scenario figure).
  • PGI: 8 × $1,500 × 12 = $144,000.
  • Vacancy and collection loss at 5 percent of PGI: $144,000 × 0.05 = $7,200.
  • PGI minus vacancy: $144,000 − $7,200 = $136,800.
  • Laundry income: $2,400 a year.
  • EGI: $136,800 + $2,400 = $139,200.
  • Operating expenses: property taxes $16,000, insurance $14,000, maintenance $9,000, management $8,000, reserves $1,000. Total $48,000.
  • The owner also pays $62,000 a year on the mortgage. Ignore it.
  • NOI: $139,200 − $48,000 = $91,200.
  • Market cap rate for similar buildings: 8 percent.
  • Value: $91,200 ÷ 0.08 = $1,140,000.

How the cap rate behaves: the cap rate and value move in opposite directions. At 7.5 percent, the same NOI gives $91,200 ÷ 0.075 = $1,216,000. At 9 percent, $91,200 ÷ 0.09 = $1,013,333. A lower cap rate means buyers accept a lower return, which means they pay more. A riskier property needs a higher cap rate.

Where the cap rate comes from: the appraiser derives it from comparable sales. A similar building that sold for $1,000,000 with an NOI of $80,000 shows a cap rate of $80,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 8 percent.

The trap: subtracting the mortgage payment. If debt service ends up in the expense line, NOI drops to $29,200 and the "value" collapses to $365,000. That number will be one of the answer choices.

Gross rent and gross income multipliers

A shortcut version of the income approach, using gross income instead of NOI and a multiplier instead of a rate.

  • Gross rent multiplier (GRM) — used for single-family homes and small residential rentals. Based on monthly rent.
  • Gross income multiplier (GIM) — used for commercial and larger income property. Based on annual gross income.

The formula in both cases: Sales price ÷ gross rent (or income) = multiplier. Then multiplier × subject's rent = value.

Worked example, GRM. The subject is a rented house at 2850 Mahogany Drive that brings $2,400 a month. Three comparable rented houses:

  • Sold for $360,000, renting for $2,400 a month: 360,000 ÷ 2,400 = 150.
  • Sold for $315,000, renting for $2,100 a month: 315,000 ÷ 2,100 = 150.
  • Sold for $372,000, renting for $2,400 a month: 372,000 ÷ 2,400 = 155.

The comps cluster around 150. Applying it: 150 × $2,400 = $360,000.

Worked example, GIM. A small strip center at 4100 Lignumvitae Street has annual gross income of $210,000. Comparable centers sold at a GIM of 7.5. Value: 7.5 × $210,000 = $1,575,000.

The trap: the time unit. GRM uses monthly rent; GIM uses annual income. A question that gives an annual figure for a house and asks for value with a GRM of 150 is checking whether the conversion happens first. The other trap: multipliers ignore expenses and vacancy entirely, which is why they are a rough tool, not a substitute for NOI.

Reconciliation and the final opinion

Reconciliation is the step where the appraiser reviews each approach, judges how reliable and relevant it is for this property, and arrives at one final opinion of value. The usual weighting:

  • Single-family homes and vacant land: sales comparison carries the most weight.
  • New or special-purpose buildings: cost approach.
  • Income-producing property: income approach.

The report states the opinion as of a specific effective date. Values change; an appraisal is a snapshot.

The trap: the word "average." Any answer choice that says the appraiser averages the three approaches is wrong.

The licensee's CMA in practice

A CMA uses the same logic as the sales comparison approach, with less rigor. A listing agent preparing a CMA for a seller at 1509 Mahogany Drive looks at:

  • Sold properties — the strongest evidence; what buyers actually paid.
  • Active listings — the competition; what the seller is up against.
  • Pending sales — what is selling right now, though the final price is not yet known.
  • Expired listings — what the market rejected, usually because the price was too high.

The CMA gives the seller a range and a recommended list price. It is advice, not an appraisal, and in Florida it must never be called one.

The trap: a seller who says "I want $520,000, and you'll put that in the CMA." The licensee may list at the seller's price — the seller decides — but cannot falsify the analysis to support it. A CMA that misstates the comps is a misrepresentation, which is a license-law violation in its own right.

How to use this chapter

Read the sections in order, then work the chapter in the course. Do each of the worked examples above once by hand before the book's practice problems — the setup of the income approach and the comp adjustments are where the exam's wrong answers come from. If a number in the textbook differs from anything here, the textbook wins.
