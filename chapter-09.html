Chapter 9 — Title, Deeds, and Ownership Restrictions

What this chapter is. Chapter 8 named the interests a person can own. This chapter is about how ownership actually moves from one person to another, how anyone proves it, and what can limit it. Three ideas carry the whole chapter: title is ownership itself, a deed is the document that transfers it, and recording is what protects the new owner against the rest of the world. The exam tests the required elements of a valid Florida deed (Florida adds a requirement most states do not), the differences between deed types, Florida's recording rule, and the long list of encumbrances that can sit on a title without anyone noticing.

Where a number below is stated, it is certain. Where it says "under Florida law" without a number, take the number from the textbook.

Title versus deed

Title is the ownership — the bundle of rights itself. It is not a piece of paper. A deed is the written instrument that transfers title from one person (the grantor) to another (the grantee).

The distinction matters because title can move without a deed (by inheritance, by court order, by adverse possession), and a deed can exist without moving title (if it was never delivered).

The trap: a seller on Banyan Terrace hands the buyer a signed deed in a sealed envelope and says "don't open it until I die." That is not delivery; the grantor kept control. Title has not passed, and the deed is just paper until it is genuinely handed over with intent to transfer.

How title is acquired

Transfers fall into two families.

Voluntary alienation is a transfer the owner chooses: a sale or gift by deed during life, or a devise by will at death. The person who leaves real property by will is the testator; the person who receives it is the devisee.

Involuntary alienation is a transfer the owner does not choose:

  • Descent (intestate succession). An owner who dies without a will has their property pass to heirs under Florida's probate code. Florida homestead rules override a will in some situations, as covered in Chapter 8.
  • Escheat. An owner who dies with no will and no heirs: the property goes to the state.
  • Eminent domain. Government takes private property for public use and must pay just compensation. The legal process is condemnation.
  • Foreclosure and other court sales. A creditor with a lien forces a sale.
  • Natural forces. Accretion is the gradual addition of land by water depositing soil (alluvion is the soil itself). Reliction is land exposed when water gradually recedes. Erosion is gradual loss. Avulsion is sudden loss, as from a hurricane; Florida generally leaves the boundary where it was before an avulsive event, so the owner does not lose title just because the land washed away overnight.
  • Adverse possession. Covered next.

The trap: avulsion versus erosion. Gradual is erosion and moves the boundary. Sudden is avulsion and generally does not.

Adverse possession in Florida

Someone who openly occupies another person's land, without permission, for long enough can acquire title. The possession has to be actual, open and notorious, hostile (without the owner's permission), exclusive, and continuous for the statutory period.

Florida's period is 7 years, and Florida has two paths:

  • With color of title: the possessor holds a written instrument — a defective deed, a flawed court judgment — that appears to give them title. The possession has to last 7 continuous years.
  • Without color of title: the possessor has no such document. In addition to 7 years of possession, Florida requires the possessor to file a return with the county property appraiser and to pay the property taxes on the land during the period. Without the tax payments, the claim fails.

Adverse possession does not run against government land.

Getting the title on record still requires a court action to quiet title — a lawsuit that declares the possessor the owner and removes the old owner's claim.

The trap: a neighbor on Snook Creek Road fences in a strip of the vacant lot next door, mows it for eight years, and claims it. Without color of title and without having paid the property taxes on that strip, under Florida law the claim fails. The number the exam wants is 7; the condition it hides is the taxes.

What makes a Florida deed valid

A valid Florida deed has these elements:

  1. In writing. Florida's statute of frauds and Section 689.01 of the Florida Statutes require conveyances of real property to be written.
  2. A competent grantor — of legal age and sound mind.
  3. A grantee who can be identified. The grantee does not have to be competent (a minor can receive title), but must exist and be named or described clearly enough to identify.
  4. Words of conveyance — the granting clause, such as "grants, bargains, sells and conveys."
  5. Consideration recited. The deed states that something was given, even if it is "ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration." A gift deed is still valid; the recital is the formality.
  6. A legal description of the property (Chapter 10).
  7. The grantor's signature. Only the grantor signs. The grantee does not.
  8. Two witnesses. This is the Florida-specific requirement. Section 689.01 requires the grantor's signature to be made in the presence of two subscribing witnesses, who also sign.
  9. Delivery and acceptance. The grantor has to hand over the deed with the intent to transfer title, and the grantee has to accept it. Title passes on delivery and acceptance, not on signing and not on recording.

Acknowledgment — the grantor declaring before a notary that the signature is theirs and was given voluntarily — is not required for the deed to be valid between the parties. It is required for the deed to be recorded in Florida.

For recording, Florida's statute on the form of recorded instruments also requires the printed names and post-office addresses of the people who sign, of each witness, and of the grantee, along with the name and address of whoever prepared the document. A deed missing them is still valid between the parties, but the clerk can refuse to record it.

The trap: the exam presents a deed signed by the grantor and notarized, with no witnesses, and asks whether it is valid in Florida. It is not — the notary does not replace the two witnesses. The second trap is the grantee's signature: a deed with only the grantor's signature is complete. A question that says "the deed is invalid because the buyer never signed it" is wrong.

The parts of the deed

The exam will name the clauses:

  • Premises: the names of the parties, the consideration, the words of conveyance, the legal description.
  • Habendum clause ("to have and to hold"): defines the estate being granted — fee simple, life estate.
  • Testimonium clause: the closing statement that the grantor has signed it, followed by signatures, witnesses and the acknowledgment.
  • Exceptions and reservations: rights the grantor keeps or that the deed is subject to — an easement, mineral rights, existing restrictions.

The trap: the habendum clause is the one that describes the estate. The granting clause is the one that transfers.

Types of deeds

The difference between deed types is entirely about what the grantor promises about the title — the warranties.

General warranty deed. The grantor warrants title against all defects, including ones that arose before the grantor owned it. It carries the standard covenants:

  • Covenant of seisin — the grantor owns the property and has the right to convey it.
  • Covenant against encumbrances — there are no liens or encumbrances except those listed.
  • Covenant of quiet enjoyment — no one with a better title will disturb the grantee's possession.
  • Covenant of further assurance — the grantor will sign whatever else is needed to perfect the title.
  • Covenant of warranty forever — the grantor will defend the title against all lawful claims, for all time.

This is the best deed a buyer can get and the standard deed in a Florida residential sale.

Special warranty deed. The grantor warrants only against defects that arose while the grantor owned the property — "by, through or under" the grantor — and not against anything earlier. Often used by banks selling foreclosed property, by developers, and by corporations.

Bargain and sale deed. Implies the grantor holds title but makes no warranties against encumbrances.

Quitclaim deed. No warranties at all. The grantor conveys whatever interest they have, if any. If they have nothing, the grantee gets nothing. Used to clear a cloud on title (an heir quitclaims a possible interest), to transfer between spouses, or to fix an error. A quitclaim deed does still transfer full title if the grantor actually has it.

Fiduciary and court deeds. A personal representative's deed conveys property from a decedent's estate; a guardian's deed from a ward's property; a trustee's deed from a trust; a tax deed after a tax-certificate sale; a sheriff's deed or certificate of title after a foreclosure or execution sale. These usually carry limited warranties or none.

The trap: the bank-owned house on Ixora Drive. The bank delivers a special warranty deed. A title defect from the previous owner, who lost the house in foreclosure, is not covered by the bank's warranty — only defects the bank itself created are. The protection for the buyer there is title insurance, not the deed.

Recording and notice

Recording means filing the deed with the Clerk of the Circuit Court (the official records) in the county where the property is located. Recording does not make a deed valid; delivery does that. Recording protects the grantee against later claims by giving the world constructive notice of the transfer.

There are two kinds of notice:

  • Constructive notice — notice the law presumes everyone has, because it is in the public record or because someone is visibly in possession of the property. Anyone dealing with the property is charged with knowing what a search of the records or an inspection would reveal.
  • Actual notice — what a person actually knows, because they saw it, were told, or read it.

Florida's recording statute is a notice statute. Under Section 695.01, an unrecorded deed is not valid against a later purchaser who pays value without notice of the earlier deed. If the later buyer had no notice and paid value, they win even if they record second — or don't record at all. If the later buyer knew about the earlier deed, or should have known (constructive notice), they lose.

Possession counts as notice. A buyer is expected to inspect the property and ask who is living there and on what basis.

The trap: a seller on Wild Coffee Lane sells to Buyer A, who does not record. The seller then sells the same house to Buyer B, who pays full price, has no idea of the first sale, and finds a vacant house. Under Florida's notice statute, B wins. Change one fact — A has moved in and is living there — and B is charged with notice through A's possession, and A wins.

Evidence of title

A buyer needs proof the seller actually owns what they are selling. There are three main forms.

Abstract of title with attorney's opinion. An abstract is a condensed history of every recorded document affecting the property — the chain of title. An attorney reviews it and gives an opinion of title on whether the title is good. The abstract is a history, not a guarantee, and it cannot reveal anything that is not in the records.

Title insurance. A title insurance company searches the records, issues a title commitment listing what it will and will not insure, and then issues a policy. An owner's policy protects the buyer, for the purchase price, as long as the buyer or heirs own the property. A lender's (mortgagee's) policy protects only the lender, for the loan balance, and decreases as the loan is paid down. A buyer whose lender requires a lender's policy is not protected by it. Title insurance can cover risks that do not appear in the records, such as forgery or an undisclosed heir, depending on the policy's terms.

Certificate of title. An attorney's or abstracter's statement, based on a review of the records, that title is good. Like the opinion, it is not insurance.

Marketable title is title free of reasonable doubt — one a reasonable buyer would accept. A cloud on title is any claim that throws doubt on it, and a quitclaim deed or a quiet-title suit is the usual way to remove one.

Florida's Marketable Record Title Act (Chapter 712) simplifies this by setting a 30-year look-back. A person with an unbroken chain of record title going back to a "root of title" at least 30 years old holds title free of most older claims that have not been preserved by re-recording. Some interests survive regardless — for example, certain government and utility interests; take the list from the book.

The trap: the buyer on Snook Creek Road who says "the lender got title insurance, so I'm covered." She is not. The lender's policy protects the lender. Only an owner's policy protects her.

Ownership restrictions: the government's four powers

Every private owner holds title subject to four government powers. The memory device is PETE:

  • Police power — the state's authority to regulate for public health, safety and welfare: zoning, building codes, subdivision rules, environmental regulation. No compensation is owed for ordinary regulation.
  • Eminent domain — the power to take private property for public use with just compensation. Florida's constitution requires full compensation and has strong owner protections, including limits on taking property to transfer it to a private party; take the specifics from the book.
  • Taxation — the power to tax real property, enforced by a lien on the property itself.
  • Escheat — property of a person who dies without a will and without heirs passes to the state.

The trap: zoning that lowers a lot's value is police power, not eminent domain, and no compensation is owed. Eminent domain is a taking, and compensation is owed.

Encumbrances: liens

An encumbrance is any claim, charge or limitation on a title that does not prevent it from being transferred but may affect its value or use. Liens are encumbrances that affect title; easements and restrictions affect use.

A lien is a claim against property as security for a debt. The exam classifies them:

  • Voluntary (the owner agreed — a mortgage) or involuntary (imposed by law — a tax lien, a judgment lien, a construction lien).
  • Specific (attaches to one property — a mortgage, a property-tax lien, a construction lien) or general (attaches to all of a debtor's property in the county — a judgment lien, a federal income-tax lien).

Property tax liens come first. In Florida, real property tax liens have priority over almost every other lien, including mortgages recorded earlier.

Special assessment liens are levied for a specific improvement that benefits particular properties — a new sidewalk, a sewer line — and are specific liens on those properties.

Judgment liens. When a creditor wins a court judgment and records a certified copy in the county's official records, it becomes a lien on the debtor's real property in that county, other than homestead.

Construction liens (Chapter 713) protect contractors, subcontractors, laborers and suppliers who improve property and are not paid. Florida's construction lien law has a sequence of notices and deadlines — the notice of commencement the owner records before work starts, the notice to owner from subcontractors and suppliers, and the claim of lien. A claim of lien must be recorded within 90 days after the claimant's final furnishing of labor or materials. Take the other deadlines from the book.

A lis pendens ("suit pending") is a recorded notice that a lawsuit affecting title has been filed. It is not a lien itself, but it warns any buyer that whatever they buy is subject to the outcome.

The trap: a homeowner on Banyan Terrace pays her general contractor in full for a kitchen remodel. The contractor never pays the cabinet supplier. The supplier, having served its notice correctly, can still record a construction lien against her house. Paying the contractor does not cut off the subcontractors' and suppliers' lien rights. That is why Florida's notice sequence exists and why the owner should collect lien releases before final payment.

Encumbrances: easements

An easement is a right to use someone else's land for a specific purpose, without possessing it.

  • Easement appurtenant involves two parcels. The dominant estate benefits; the servient estate carries the burden. The easement "runs with the land" — it passes automatically to future owners of both parcels.
  • Easement in gross benefits a person or company, not a neighboring parcel. Utility easements are the common example — the power company's right to run lines along the back of every lot.
  • Easement by necessity arises when a parcel is landlocked — no access to a public road — because of how the land was divided. The law implies a right to cross the parcel that was carved away from it.
  • Easement by prescription arises from open, continuous, adverse use for the prescriptive period without the owner's permission. In Florida that period is 20 years. Unlike adverse possession, the user gets a right to use, not ownership, and there is no tax-payment requirement.

Easements can end by merger (one person comes to own both parcels), by release, by abandonment, or when the purpose ends.

A license is personal permission to use land, revocable at will and not an interest in the land. A ticket to a concert or permission to fish in a neighbor's pond is a license.

The trap: the neighbor on Ixora Drive who has walked across the back of the next lot to reach the canal for 10 years. That is not enough for a prescriptive easement in Florida — the period is 20. And the other trap: a landlocked lot owner has an easement by necessity only if the landlocking came from a common owner's split of the land.

Encumbrances: encroachments and deed restrictions

An encroachment is something that physically intrudes onto someone else's property — a fence, a driveway, a roof overhang. It is usually discovered by a survey, not by a title search, which is why a buyer who skips the survey may never know about it. An encroachment can become the basis of an adverse possession or prescriptive claim if it goes unchallenged long enough.

Deed restrictions (restrictive covenants) are private limits placed on use — minimum house size, no commercial use, architectural approval. They may be in an individual deed or in a recorded declaration that binds a whole subdivision. They are enforced by neighbors or an HOA, not by the government. When a deed restriction and a zoning ordinance conflict, the more restrictive one controls.

Restrictions that discriminate on the basis of a protected class are unenforceable under fair housing law, even if they are still printed in an old recorded declaration.

The trap: zoning on Wild Coffee Lane allows a duplex. The subdivision's recorded covenants allow only single-family homes. The covenants control, because they are more restrictive. Zoning permission does not override a private restriction.

How to use this chapter

Read the deed sections before opening the chapter and hold onto two Florida facts: two witnesses to make the deed valid, notary to make it recordable. Then read the recording section and the encumbrance sections right before those parts of the chapter; the exam's hardest questions here are fact patterns, so pay attention to which single fact flips the answer.
