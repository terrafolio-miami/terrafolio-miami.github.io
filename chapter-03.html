Study companion — Chapter 3: License Law and Commission Rules

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course, Chapter 3. Pre-reading: go through a section here, then open that part of the chapter, so the book reads as a second pass instead of a cold start.

What this chapter is. Chapter 2 was about getting a license. This chapter is about the machinery that keeps it: who regulates Florida real estate licensees, where the rules live, what states a license can be in, how it is renewed, what a licensee has to tell the state and when, and how brokerages that are companies rather than people get on the books. The exam cares because almost every question here has one right answer and three answers that are each a small, believable error — the wrong agency, the wrong status, the wrong person who has to be licensed. Brokerage procedures, escrow, violations and penalties are Chapters 5 and 6, not here.

Everything below is Florida law and Florida practice: Chapter 475 of the Florida Statutes, the general professional-regulation statute in Chapter 455, and the Commission's rules. Where a number is stated, it is certain. Where it is not, it says "under Florida law" — take the number from the textbook.

Two bodies, one job: DBPR and FREC

Florida splits the regulation of real estate licensees between two bodies, and the exam asks which one does what.

The Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) is a state agency. It regulates dozens of professions, not just real estate, and it is headed by a Secretary appointed by the Governor. Inside DBPR is the Division of Real Estate, which does the administrative work for real estate: it processes applications, issues and renews licenses, keeps the licensing records, collects the fees, and — through DBPR — investigates complaints. DBPR is the staff, the files and the investigators.

The Florida Real Estate Commission (FREC) is the board that sets policy and makes decisions. It has seven members, appointed by the Governor and confirmed by the Senate. Four must be licensed brokers, one must be a licensed broker or sales associate, and two must be consumer members who are not and have never been brokers or sales associates. At least one member must be 60 years of age or older. Each of the licensed seats also carries a minimum period of active licensure before appointment, and members serve fixed terms; take those figures from the textbook.

The textbooks describe FREC's authority as three kinds of power:

  • Executive (administrative) power — carrying out and enforcing the license law: deciding who qualifies, approving education, regulating licensees' conduct.
  • Quasi-legislative power — making rules. FREC cannot pass a statute, but it can adopt rules that implement the statute, and those rules have the force of law.
  • Quasi-judicial power — deciding discipline. When a case reaches the Commission, FREC acts like a court: it decides whether a violation occurred and what penalty applies.

Why Florida does it this way: the profession knows the business, so licensees sit on the board that writes the rules and judges conduct — but the consumer seats and the separate state agency keep the profession from regulating only itself. DBPR handles the paperwork and investigations so the Commission can concentrate on policy and decisions.

Scene: a buyer on Paurotis Drive thinks a listing agent pocketed part of her deposit. She files the complaint with DBPR. A DBPR investigator gathers the documents and interviews. Whether the agent is ultimately disciplined, and how severely, is a FREC decision. The path between is Chapter 6.

The trap: "FREC investigates complaints" or "DBPR revokes licenses." Both reverse the roles. DBPR investigates and administers; FREC makes rules and imposes discipline. The other trap is the membership: the exam will offer "five brokers and two consumers" or "seven brokers." Four brokers, one broker or sales associate, two consumer members.

Where the rules live: Chapter 475 and Rule 61J2

There are three layers of law in this chapter, and it helps to keep them straight.

Chapter 475, Florida Statutes, is the real estate license law itself, passed by the Legislature.

Chapter 455, Florida Statutes, is the general law for all professions regulated by DBPR. A good deal of what governs license status, renewal and address changes comes from here, because it applies to every DBPR licensee, not just real estate.

Chapter 61J2 of the Florida Administrative Code is FREC's own rule book. It fills in the detail the statute leaves open: forms, education, office, advertising and escrow specifics. FREC adopts these rules through the process set by Florida's Administrative Procedure Act, Chapter 120 of the Florida Statutes, which requires public notice and a chance for the public to comment before a rule takes effect.

Why it matters: breaking a Commission rule is itself a violation of the license law, even if the statute never mentions the specific conduct.

Scene: a sales associate at a brokerage on Pigeon Plum Street reads Chapter 475 front to back, finds nothing about exactly how a trade name must appear on a sign, and decides there is no requirement. The requirement is there; it is in the rules, not the statute.

The trap: treating the rules as weaker than the statute. A rule cannot override a statute, but within its scope it binds licensees exactly as the statute does. A second trap: the exam may ask who adopts the rules. FREC does.

What a license can be: active, inactive, null and void

A Florida real estate license is always in one of a few statuses, and the exam tests what moves a license from one to another.

Active. The licensee may perform real estate services for compensation. For a sales associate or broker associate, active status requires being registered under an employing broker (or an owner-developer, covered below). A sales associate with no employing broker cannot be active, no matter how current the renewal and the education are.

Voluntary inactive. The licensee chose to stop practicing and asked for inactive status. The license still exists. The licensee keeps renewing it and keeps up the continuing education, and can return to active status by meeting the requirements and, for an associate, registering with a broker.

Involuntary inactive. The license was not renewed on time. Under Chapter 475, any license — active or inactive — that is not renewed at the end of its license period automatically becomes involuntarily inactive. An involuntarily inactive license can be brought back within 2 years by completing the reactivation education the Commission requires and paying the fees. The amount of education depends on how long the license has been involuntarily inactive; take the hour counts from the textbook.

Null and void. The license no longer exists. A license that stays involuntarily inactive past that 2-year window becomes null and void, and the person has to start over — the pre-license course, the application and the state exam — to practice again. A license can also become null and void in other ways the chapter lists, the most important for a new sales associate being failure to complete post-licensing education, covered in the next section.

Why Florida does it this way: inactive status lets a licensee step away without losing what they earned, as long as they keep renewing. Involuntary inactive is a grace period for someone who simply missed a deadline. Null and void is the line after which the state no longer assumes the person's knowledge is current.

Scene: a sales associate living on Simpson Stopper Way takes a year off to care for a parent. She asks for inactive status, renews on time and keeps her education current. Her license is voluntary inactive, and she can return when ready. Her colleague forgets to renew at all. His license became involuntarily inactive the day after it expired; if two years pass without reactivation, it is null and void.

The trap: "inactive" as one word. The exam distinguishes voluntary from involuntary by cause — choice versus failure to renew. A licensee who simply let the renewal pass did not "choose" inactive status, even if he meant to quit. Second trap: an involuntarily inactive licensee who keeps showing houses is practicing without an active license, which is a violation. And the related status words — void, ineffective — come up again in Chapter 6, where a sales associate whose broker is suspended keeps a license that exists but cannot be used.

Renewal and education across the license cycle

Chapter 2 introduced the education requirements. This chapter ties them to the renewal cycle and to the statuses above.

Florida real estate licenses run on a two-year (biennial) license period, and licenses expire on either March 31 or September 30. The licensee renews before the expiration date by paying the fee and certifying the required education. The first license period after initial licensure is shorter than a full two years; take how it is set from the textbook.

What each renewal requires depends on where the licensee is in the cycle:

  • First renewal. A sales associate must have completed the 45-hour post-licensing course (a broker, 60 hours). Missing it does not lead to involuntary inactive. The license becomes null and void. This is not discipline; the license simply ends, and the textbook covers what has to be retaken.
  • Every renewal after that. 14 hours of continuing education each license period. Under Florida law a set portion must be core law, and the Commission requires other specific content as well; take the breakdown of the 14 from the textbook.
  • Inactive licensees. A voluntary inactive license still has to be renewed with the education done. Skipping it does not keep the license safely in storage.

Why Florida does it this way: post-licensing is the second half of a new licensee's basic education, so Florida will not let a license into its second cycle without it. After that, the law, the forms and the market keep changing, and a two-year cycle with a core-law component means every licensee looks at current law at least that often.

Scene: two licensees at the Paurotis Drive office share a September 30 expiration. One is a broker associate who, on September 12, has finished 11 of her 14 hours (11 is a scenario figure). If she does not finish and renew by September 30, on October 1 her license is involuntarily inactive and can be reactivated. The other is a sales associate licensed the previous year who never started the 45-hour course. On October 1 his license is null and void.

The trap: confusing the three courses. Pre-licensing (63 hours) gets you to the exam. Post-licensing (45 for a sales associate) is due before the first renewal, and missing it voids the license. Continuing education (14 hours) is due at every renewal after that, and missing it makes the license involuntarily inactive. Second trap: renewal is due by the expiration date, not shortly after it. A license not renewed on time is involuntarily inactive the next day.

Changes of address and employer

The state has to be able to find every licensee, and every sales associate has to be traceable to the broker responsible for them. Two notice obligations follow.

Change of address. A licensee who changes their current mailing address must notify the Commission within 10 days. Florida law treats notices sent by DBPR to the last address on file as properly served — so a licensee who moves and does not update the record can miss a complaint, a renewal notice or a hearing date and still be bound by what was sent.

Change of employer. A sales associate or broker associate who leaves one broker and goes to work for another must notify the Commission of the change, within 10 days, using the process DBPR provides. Until the change is recorded, the associate is not properly registered with the new broker. The broker side matters too: when a broker moves the brokerage office, the broker notifies the Commission, and the change carries over to every associate registered at that office.

Why: Chapter 5 will show that the broker is legally responsible for every associate's conduct and every dollar the associates handle. The registration record is how the state knows which broker that is on any given day.

Scene: a sales associate leaves the Pigeon Plum Street brokerage on a Friday, starts at a firm on Simpson Stopper Way the next Monday, and plans to "get to the paperwork." On Wednesday he writes an offer. If the change is not recorded, he is not registered with the broker whose name is on that offer.

The trap: thinking an address change only matters for mail. It matters for service of legal notices — not receiving a complaint because it went to an old address is not a defense. A second trap: the associate may not keep working for clients of the old broker after leaving. The listings and the clients belong to the broker, not the associate.

Registering a brokerage that is a company

Only individuals take the exam and hold a license. A brokerage that is a business entity — a corporation, a general or limited partnership, a limited liability company, a limited liability partnership — is registered with the Commission. Registration is what lets the entity itself act as a broker.

Florida's basic principle is that a licensed active broker must be at the top of every registered entity:

  • A corporation must have at least one officer or director who is an active broker.
  • A general partnership must have at least one partner who is an active broker.
  • A limited partnership must have at least one general partner who is an active broker. Limited partners, who invest but do not manage, do not have to be licensed.
  • A limited liability company or limited liability partnership must have at least one member, manager or partner who is an active broker, in the role the textbook describes for each.

Beyond that one required broker, the rule that ties it together: anyone in the entity who performs real estate services for others must hold the right license. Owning shares in a brokerage does not require a license. Showing property and negotiating a sale for a fee does. And holding the officer, director or general-partner positions that run a brokerage is a broker's role; under Florida law a sales associate may not hold those positions in the brokerage they work for. The textbook lists exactly which positions are covered.

The entity also has to exist under Florida's general business law — filed with the Florida Department of State — before it can be registered with the Commission. Registration with FREC does not create the company; it lets an existing company broker.

Why: the public deals with a company, but the accountability has to rest with a person who passed the broker exam. Requiring an active broker at the top means there is always a licensed individual answerable for what the entity does.

Scene: three friends form an LLC to open a brokerage on Necklace Pod Lane. One is a broker; the other two are a sales associate and an investor who has never held a license. The broker is the required licensed member or manager. The investor may own a share without a license, as long as she never performs brokerage services. The sales associate can work for the brokerage as an associate, but cannot be the one who runs it.

The trap: "the company is licensed." Companies are registered; people are licensed. The exam uses those two words carefully. A second trap: the investor who owns half the company and starts "helping out" by negotiating a deal for a commission. Ownership did not require a license; that activity does.

Trade names

A broker may operate under a trade name rather than their own name: a broker doing business as "Pigeon Plum Realty" instead of under her personal name, for example. The trade name has to be registered with the Commission and appears on the license, and it then has to be used consistently — on the office sign and in advertising, as Chapter 5 covers. Using a name the Commission has no record of is a violation, because the public cannot connect the name to the accountable broker. Florida's fictitious-name registration with the Department of State is a separate filing from the Commission registration; one does not substitute for the other.

A sales associate cannot have a trade name for their brokerage business, because a sales associate cannot operate a brokerage. Team names and personal branding are an advertising question, handled in Chapter 5, and they must always appear alongside the brokerage's registered name.

The textbook covers limits on what a trade name may say, such as names that mislead about who is behind the business.

Why: the trade name is the public's link to the license. If the name on the sign does not match the name on file, the public has no way to check who is responsible.

Scene: a broker on Paurotis Drive registers her brokerage as "Paurotis Coastal Realty." Two years later she starts advertising as "Paurotis Luxury Homes" without registering the new name. The new name may be a better brand. Until it is registered, using it is the violation.

The trap: "a sales associate may register a trade name if the broker approves." No. The license and the business belong to the broker; the associate's name appears only under the broker's.

Multiple licenses and group licenses

As a general rule, a sales associate or broker associate works for one employer at a time and holds one license under that employer.

A broker may hold multiple licenses — a separate license for each registered brokerage entity in which the broker serves as the required active broker. A broker who is an officer of two separate brokerage corporations needs a license for each. This is not the same as branch offices, which are extra locations of one brokerage.

The one exception for associates is the group license. An owner-developer — a company that owns properties and sells or rents them, often through a family of related entities (a parent company and its subsidiaries, or several entities under common ownership) — may employ a sales associate to sell or rent property owned by all of those related entities. Instead of a separate employer registration for each, the associate holds a group license covering the group. It applies only to properties the related entities own; it does not let the associate broker for outside owners.

Why: a sales associate who could work for several unrelated brokers at once would have no single broker responsible for their conduct or the money they handle. The group license works only because the entities are related and owned in common, so there is still one identifiable chain of responsibility.

Scene: a developer has three related companies building three subdivisions — one off Simpson Stopper Way, one off Necklace Pod Lane, one off Pigeon Plum Street. One sales associate sells homes in all three. A group license covers her for all three entities. If an unrelated homeowner down the road asks her to list a resale, the group license does not cover it.

The trap: "a sales associate may work for two brokers if both consent." No, consent does not create a second employer. The only route to more than one employer for an associate is a group license, and only for related owner-developer entities. The second trap: mixing up multiple licenses (a broker's, for separate brokerages) with a group license (an associate's, for related owner-developers).

How to use this chapter

Read the sections in order, then work the chapter in the course. This chapter is mostly classification — which agency, which status, which person, which license — so as you read the book, keep sorting each fact into one of those boxes. Write down every number the textbook gives that this companion sent you to find: FREC term lengths, the reactivation hour counts, the breakdown of the 14 hours, the length of the first license period. If a number in the textbook differs from anything here, the textbook wins.
