Study companion — Chapter 4: Authorized Relationships, Duties and Disclosure

Gold Coast 63-hour Sales Associate course, Chapter 4. Pre-reading: go through a section here, then open that part of the chapter, so the book reads as a second pass instead of a cold start.

What this chapter is. Who a licensee works for, what they owe each person in the deal, and how they have to tell people about it. Florida answers those questions in Chapter 475 of the Florida Statutes, through the part known as the Brokerage Relationship Disclosure Act. The Act lists the relationships a Florida licensee may have with a buyer or seller. It sets out the duties that come with each one, word for word. And it says which transactions need a written disclosure and when.

The exam cares because this is where Florida departs most from the generic real estate textbook. Generic agency law assumes the listing agent is the seller's agent and talks about dual agency as a disclosed option. Florida presumes something else, and it forbids dual agency outright. Most of the chapter's questions are fact patterns. The job is to name the relationship, then name the duty that was or was not owed.

Everything below is Florida law. Where a number is stated, it is certain. Where it says "under Florida law" without one, take the number from the textbook.

Agency basics: principal, customer and fiduciary

An agent acts on behalf of a principal, and agency makes the agent a fiduciary: someone trusted to put the principal's interests ahead of their own and everyone else's. A customer is anyone in the transaction who is not the principal. The licensee deals with a customer honestly but does not work for them.

Agency can be express (a stated agreement) or implied (inferred from conduct). Florida narrows this: single agency has to be established in writing. A buyer who talks to a licensee for weeks and comes to believe that licensee is "his agent" does not have one without the written disclosure. He is far more likely to be dealing with a transaction broker.

Agency ends when the job is done, the agreement expires, the parties agree, one withdraws, a party dies or becomes incapacitated, or the property is destroyed. Confidentiality survives the ending.

The trap: the casual word "agent." Under Chapter 475 what matters is which authorized relationship the licensee is in, and fiduciary duties come only from single agency.

General, special and universal agents

These labels are about scope of authority.

  • A universal agent can do essentially anything the principal could do. A broad, durable power of attorney is the usual example. Rare in brokerage.
  • A general agent has ongoing authority across a continuing course of business. A sales associate is a general agent of the employing broker. A property manager who collects rent, signs leases and hires repairs year after year is another.
  • A special agent is hired for one specific task, and the authority ends when the task does. A broker who takes one listing is a special agent of that seller.

Because the sales associate is the broker's general agent, the broker answers for the associate's conduct in the broker's business.

The trap: the listing broker is a special agent of the seller, and the sales associate is a general agent of the broker, both at once and both in the same deal. A question that asks "what kind of agent is the sales associate who took the listing on 2210 Fiddlewood Avenue?" wants "general agent of the broker."

The three authorized relationships, and whose relationship it is

Florida allows exactly three relationships between a licensee and a buyer or seller:

  1. Transaction broker: limited representation to the buyer, the seller, or both.
  2. Single agent: full fiduciary representation of one party, either the buyer or the seller, never both in the same transaction.
  3. No brokerage relationship: the licensee represents no one.

Nonresidential deals add one more arrangement, designated sales associates, covered below.

Every relationship is between the brokerage and the customer, not between the sales associate personally and the customer. The associate acts in the broker's name and is paid only by the broker. So when one associate in a firm is a single agent for a seller, the whole firm is in that relationship with the seller. A second associate in the same office cannot be a single agent for the buyer of that same property.

The trap: treating the associate as the party to the relationship. A question that says "the sales associate decided to become the buyer's single agent while her colleague kept the listing" describes something the firm cannot do in a residential sale. The firm, not the person, holds the relationship.

Transaction broker, and the presumption

A transaction broker provides limited representation to a buyer, a seller, or both. The parties are customers, not principals. They give up undivided loyalty; in exchange the licensee can help both sides close without being an advocate against either.

The presumption. Under Chapter 475, all licensees are presumed to be operating as transaction brokers unless a single agent or no brokerage relationship is established, in writing, with a customer. No written disclosure is required to be a transaction broker. It is the default you fall into by doing nothing.

The statute lists the transaction broker's duties:

  1. Dealing honestly and fairly.
  2. Accounting for all funds.
  3. Using skill, care and diligence in the transaction.
  4. Disclosing all known facts that materially affect the value of residential real property and are not readily observable to the buyer.
  5. Presenting all offers and counteroffers in a timely manner, unless a party has previously directed the licensee otherwise in writing.
  6. Limited confidentiality, unless waived in writing by a party.
  7. Any additional duties that are mutually agreed to with a party.

Limited confidentiality has a defined scope. The transaction broker may not disclose any of these:

  • that the seller will accept a price less than the asking or listed price
  • that the buyer will pay a price greater than the price in a written offer
  • either party's motivation for buying or selling
  • that either party will agree to financing terms other than those offered
  • any other information a party asks to keep confidential

Missing from the list, compared with a single agent: loyalty, obedience and full disclosure. The transaction broker takes orders from neither party and advocates for neither.

A scene. A licensee works with both the seller of 418 Saw Palmetto Trail, listed at $465,000, and a buyer from her own open house (scenario figures). The seller has said privately that $440,000 would be fine. The buyer has said privately that he could stretch to $455,000. As a transaction broker she writes up the buyer's offer at $435,000 and presents it promptly. Neither private number crosses the table. She also doesn't advise the seller to hold out, because that would be advocating for one side.

The trap: confusing "limited confidentiality" with "limited honesty." The transaction broker keeps price, terms and motive back. They never keep back a known material fact about the property, and they still owe honest dealing to both parties. A second trap: a question that describes no written disclosure at all and asks for the relationship. The answer is transaction broker, because of the presumption.

Single agent and its nine duties

A single agent represents either the buyer or the seller, but not both, in the same transaction. The represented party is the principal. Everyone else is a customer. Single agency must be established in writing through the single agent notice (timing covered below).

The statute lists the single agent's duties:

  1. Dealing honestly and fairly.
  2. Loyalty.
  3. Confidentiality.
  4. Obedience.
  5. Full disclosure.
  6. Accounting for all funds.
  7. Skill, care and diligence in the transaction.
  8. Presenting all offers and counteroffers in a timely manner, unless a party has previously directed the licensee otherwise in writing.
  9. Disclosing all known facts that materially affect the value of residential real property and are not readily observable.

What each fiduciary duty means in practice:

  • Loyalty: put the principal first, ahead of your own commission.
  • Confidentiality: the principal's price, motive and private circumstances stay private. This duty outlasts the relationship.
  • Obedience: follow the principal's lawful instructions, even unwise ones. An unlawful instruction is refused, and if the principal insists, the licensee withdraws.
  • Full disclosure: tell the principal everything that bears on the deal, including what the customer lets slip and the licensee's own interest, such as a relative on the other side.

A scene. A broker's firm takes a single-agent listing on 1307 Spanish Stopper Drive. A buyer walks through with the listing associate and, without being asked, says he has already sold his house and must close fast. He is a customer. The associate owes the seller full disclosure, so that fact goes to the seller. The seller may now hold firm on price because the buyer has no time to look elsewhere. The associate still owes the buyer honesty and disclosure of known material defects. She may not tell him the house has no flooding history if she knows it does.

The trap: the exam pairs duties against each other. Obedience never excuses dishonesty, and confidentiality never covers a known material defect. A seller who says "don't mention the cracked slab" has given an instruction the single agent can't follow. Being friendly to a customer also never turns him into a principal. Only the written single agency does that.

Consent to transition to transaction broker

Florida lets a single agent change to a transaction broker during the relationship, but only with the principal's written consent before the change. The consent uses wording set out in the statute, which the textbook reproduces. It can be given at the start of the single agency, often signed along with the listing or buyer agreement, or at any later time before the change.

Why Florida built this: in-house sales. A firm with a single-agent listing often finds its own buyer. It can't represent both sides as a fiduciary, because that would be dual agency. So it steps down to transaction broker for both, with the principal's written agreement first.

A scene. A brokerage is a single agent for the seller of 905 Firewheel Road. A buyer who has been working with a different associate in the same office wants to make an offer. If the seller signed the consent to transition, the firm becomes a transaction broker. Loyalty, obedience and full disclosure fall away for the seller, and limited confidentiality takes their place. If the seller didn't sign it, the firm must get written consent before changing roles, or treat the buyer as a customer while staying the seller's single agent.

The reverse move needs no special consent form. Going from transaction broker to single agent just means establishing single agency in writing, through the single agent notice, which is how single agency is always created.

The trap: the word "before." Consent obtained after the firm has already been acting as a transaction broker for both sides doesn't cure the period it was acting without it. Nor does the transition create dual agency; it exists so the firm never becomes one.

No brokerage relationship

A licensee can deal with a buyer or seller while representing no one in the transaction. One example: a licensee brings a buyer to a for-sale-by-owner house, has no agreement with the owner, and the buyer does not want representation either. This relationship has to be established in writing with the no brokerage relationship notice. The textbook gives the exact timing of that notice. Take it from there.

The duties owed with no brokerage relationship:

  1. Dealing honestly and fairly.
  2. Disclosing all known facts that materially affect the value of residential real property and are not readily observable to the buyer.
  3. Accounting for all funds entrusted to the licensee.

That's the full list. No confidentiality of any kind, and no loyalty or obedience.

The trap: "no brokerage relationship" read as "no duties." The three that remain are the ones every relationship carries: honesty, accounting and disclosure of known material defects in residential property. Those three appear in all three lists.

Designated sales associates (nonresidential only)

In a nonresidential transaction, Florida allows a broker to designate one sales associate to act as single agent for the buyer and another to act as single agent for the seller, in the same deal, inside the same firm. Each designated sales associate has the duties of a single agent toward the party they are assigned to.

The conditions are strict. The arrangement is for nonresidential transactions only. The buyer and seller must each request it in writing, and each must meet an asset threshold that the statute sets. Take the figure from the textbook. The textbook covers the required written disclosures that go with it.

Why Florida allows it: sophisticated commercial parties want their own advocates even inside one large firm, and the law treats them as able to protect themselves.

The trap: carrying this into a residential sale. Designated sales associates are never available for a residential sale. A question set at a three-bedroom house on Fiddlewood Avenue that offers "designated sales associates" as an answer is offering a wrong answer.

Dual agency: not an authorized relationship

A dual agent is a broker who represents both the prospective buyer and the prospective seller as a fiduciary in the same transaction. Florida does not permit it, whether disclosed or not. Many other states allow disclosed dual agency with the consent of both parties, and that is exactly why the exam asks about it.

The reasoning is simple. Loyalty to the seller means getting the highest price. Loyalty to the buyer means paying the lowest. One fiduciary can't owe both at once. Florida's answer is the transaction broker: limited representation of both sides, with duties written so that neither side gets less than honest treatment and neither side gets an advocate against the other.

The trap: a fact pattern where both parties "agreed in writing" to let one licensee be a full agent for both. Consent doesn't make dual agency lawful in Florida. The correct answer is that the licensee should be a transaction broker, through the consent to transition if single agency was already in place.

Which transactions need disclosure

The disclosure requirements of the Act apply to residential sales. Under Florida law a residential sale means:

  • the sale of improved residential property of four or fewer units
  • the sale of unimproved residential property intended for use as four or fewer units
  • the sale of agricultural property of 10 or fewer acres

A five-unit building or a strip center is outside the definition. Honesty and fair dealing still apply.

The disclosure requirements also do not apply to:

  • Nonresidential transactions.
  • Rentals and leases, unless an option to purchase all or part of the property is included.
  • Bona fide open houses and model home showings that don't involve eliciting confidential information, signing a contract or representation agreement, or negotiating price, terms or conditions.
  • Unanticipated casual conversations that don't involve those same things.
  • Responding to general factual questions about properties that have been advertised for sale.
  • Communications limited to general factual information about the licensee's or firm's qualifications, background and services.
  • Auctions.
  • Appraisals.
  • Dispositions of interests in business enterprises or business opportunities, except for property with four or fewer residential units.

The trap: the open house on Saw Palmetto Trail. Greeting visitors and handing out the property sheet needs no notice. The moment the licensee asks a visitor what he could really afford, or starts writing up an offer at the kitchen counter, the exemption is gone. The exemptions are defined by what the licensee doesn't do.

When the notice is given, and how long it is kept

The single agent notice must be given before, or at the time of, entering into a listing agreement or an agreement for representation, or before the showing of property, whichever occurs first. "Whichever occurs first" is what the exam tests. A buyer's licensee who shows a buyer the house on Spanish Stopper Drive on Saturday and plans to sign the representation agreement on Monday was required to give the notice before Saturday's showing.

The transaction broker relationship needs no notice, because of the presumption. The no brokerage relationship notice is required in writing. Take its timing from the textbook. The consent to transition must be signed before the change from single agent to transaction broker.

The statutory forms have required wording, which the textbook reproduces.

Record-keeping. The broker must keep the brokerage relationship disclosure documents for 5 years for all residential transactions that result in a written contract to purchase and sell real property. Take any broader record-keeping rules from the textbook.

The trap: "a transaction broker must disclose in writing before showing property." No. Older forms show a transaction broker notice, which is why the wrong answer looks familiar. Under current Florida law, transaction broker is the presumed relationship and needs no written disclosure.

Material defects: what every licensee owes the buyer

Every relationship carries the same core disclosure duty. The licensee must disclose all known facts that materially affect the value of residential real property and are not readily observable to the buyer. Each word in that sentence does work:

  • Known: what the licensee actually knows. It isn't a duty to inspect like a home inspector.
  • Materially affect the value: a dripping faucet isn't material. A roof that leaks into the attic, a history of sinkhole activity, an unpermitted addition or recurring flooding can be.
  • Not readily observable: a stained ceiling the buyer can see from the hallway is observable. A leak painted over the weekend before the listing is not.

The licensee's duty parallels the seller's. In Johnson v. Davis (1985), the Florida Supreme Court held that a seller of residential property who knows of facts that materially affect its value, that aren't readily observable, and that aren't known to the buyer has a duty to disclose them. An "as is" clause doesn't excuse that duty. Chapter 11 covers the contract side; here the point is that the licensee can't be the channel for a seller's concealment. Florida courts have treated commercial sales differently, closer to "buyer beware." Take the textbook's framing of that boundary.

Two consequences for the licensee:

  1. A seller's instruction to keep a known defect quiet is not a lawful instruction. Obedience doesn't apply, and a single agent who follows it violates license law along with the seller.
  2. Confidentiality covers price, terms and motive. It never covers a fact about the property. The seller's reason for moving is confidential. The seller's knowledge that the pool deck is settling is not.

A scene. The seller of 2416 Fiddlewood Avenue tells the listing licensee that the back bedroom flooded in each of the last two big storms, and that new flooring and baseboards have hidden the damage. The house is listed "as is" at $389,000 (scenario figure). A buyer tours it and asks no questions about water. The licensee must disclose the flooding history. It is known, material and hidden. "As is" doesn't change that, and neither does the buyer's silence. If the seller forbids it, staying in the deal and keeping quiet is not an option.

What the licensee doesn't have to disclose under this duty: facts that aren't material, facts that are readily observable, and facts the licensee doesn't know. Florida also treats certain non-physical facts, such as a death having occurred on the property, as not material by statute. Take the precise list from the textbook.

The trap: "the buyer had an inspection, so the licensee was off the hook." An inspection that missed a hidden defect doesn't cancel the licensee's duty to disclose what the licensee knew. A second trap: the answer choice saying a transaction broker or a licensee with no brokerage relationship doesn't owe this duty. All three relationships owe it.

How to use this chapter

Read the three relationship sections first and put the three duty lists side by side on paper. Mark the three duties that appear in all of them. Mark what a single agent adds: loyalty, obedience, full disclosure and full confidentiality. Then work every fact pattern by asking two questions in order: which relationship is this, and which duty is in play. Memorize the single agent notice timing and the residential sale definition outright. If a number or form detail in the textbook differs from anything here, the textbook wins.
